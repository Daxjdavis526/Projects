## 1. Undergraduate physics curriculum relevant to GR (course sequence; when GR is first taken; published syllabi)

### Takeaway
Undergraduate GR is usually a one-term junior/senior elective that needs intermediate classical mechanics. Hartle's *Gravity* is the standard text, with Schutz as the alternative. Graduate GR (MIT 8.962, Caltech Ph 236) also expects linear algebra, differential equations and upper-level E&M. Many schools do not offer an undergraduate GR course at all.

### Cited Findings
**When GR is first taken, and what it needs**
- Hartle (UCSB) argues for an "intermediate (junior/senior) course in general relativity" and says "every undergraduate physics major should have an opportunity" to take one. — [Hartle, "General Relativity in the Undergraduate Physics Curriculum," gr-qc/0506075 (AJP 2006)](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/gr-qc/0506075)
- Hartle's prerequisites for the course:
  - "An intermediate course in mechanics" is the only essential physics prerequisite.
  - "Neither quantum mechanics nor electrodynamics are necessary."
  - The new mathematics is the metric, four-vectors and geodesics. "Only one tensor — the metric — is ever used." — [Hartle 2005](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/gr-qc/0506075)
- Hartle's "physics-first" order has four steps:
  1. Show the simplest important spacetime geometries without derivation.
  2. Derive orbits of particles and light rays (stationary proper time, effective potentials).
  3. Apply them to astrophysics and observations.
  4. Only then motivate and solve the Einstein equation.
  - His UCSB version is a one-quarter elective of about 28 lectures. — [Hartle 2005](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/gr-qc/0506075)
- An informal survey by William Hiscock of 32 Midwestern research universities found "only a handful" offering an intermediate (junior/senior) GR course. This is old (circa 2005) and informal. — [Hartle 2005](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/gr-qc/0506075)
- Hartle's *Gravity: An Introduction to Einstein's General Relativity*:
  - Described as a one-semester undergraduate text with "only introductory mechanics as a prerequisite."
  - A Physics Today review called it written for "senior level undergraduate physics students."
  - First published by Pearson Education in 2003; Cambridge University Press reissued it in 2021 (ISBN 9781316517543, about 602 pp). — [Foyles listing](https://www.foyles.co.uk/book/gravity/james-b-hartle/9781316517543); [Cambridge Bookshop](https://cambridgebookshop.co.uk/products/gravity); [NAOJ library record](https://libopac.mtk.nao.ac.jp/opac/opac_link/bibid/B000050421)

**Example undergraduate GR courses**
- Cornell PHYS/ASTRO 4445 (introductory GR, one semester):
  - Taught "at the level of" Hartle.
  - Prerequisites: classical mechanics (PHYS 3318) and special relativity (PHYS 1116/2216).
  - E&M (PHYS 3327) is "useful" but not required. — [Cornell class roster FA21](https://classes.cornell.edu/browse/roster/FA21/class/PHYS/4445)
- Georgia Tech PHYS 4147 uses Hartle's textbook ("taught with a 'physics' first approach"). Its prerequisite is PHYS 3123 Classical Mechanics I. — [GT CETL syllabus (Shoemaker)](https://cetl.gatech.edu/sites/default/files/documents/phys_4147_deirdre_shoemaker.pdf) (the link now redirects to a 404; details are from the search snippet)
- Schutz, *A First Course in General Relativity*:
  - 3rd edition, Cambridge University Press, 2022 (ISBN 9781108492676, about 512 pp).
  - Aimed at advanced undergraduates.
  - Adds two chapters on gravitational waves, including the direct detections.
  - Has over 300 exercises. — [Cambridge Higher Education page](https://www.cambridge.org/highereducation/isbn/9781108610865); [Falter listing](https://shop.falter.at/detail/9781108492676/a-first-course-in-general-relativity)
  - Older edition listings describe its audience as undergraduates "who have studied special relativity, vector calculus, and electrostatics." — [Iberlibro listing](https://www.iberlibro.com/9780521277037/First-Course-General-Relativity-Bernard-0521277035/plp)

**MIT sequence (all on OpenCourseWare)**
- 8.033 *Introduction to Relativity and Spacetime Physics* (Fall 2024 OCW):
  - Prerequisites: 18.02 Multivariable Calculus and 8.02 E&M.
  - Mostly special relativity, with a brief introduction to GR. — [MIT OCW 8.033 F24 syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-033-introduction-to-relativity-and-spacetime-physics-fall-2024/pages/syllabus)
- 8.286 *The Early Universe* (Alan Guth; Fall 2013 OCW):
  - Prerequisites: 18.03 Differential Equations and 8.02 E&M.
  - Topics include special relativity, Newtonian cosmology, an introduction to non-Euclidean spaces, big bang nucleosynthesis and inflation. — [MIT OCW 8.286 syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-286-the-early-universe-fall-2013/pages/syllabus/)
- 8.962 *General Relativity* (graduate; Scott Hughes, Spring 2020):
  - Prerequisites: 18.03 (differential equations), 18.06 (linear algebra) and 8.07 (Electromagnetism II).
  - Text: Carroll, *Spacetime and Geometry*.
  - Covers differential geometry, experimental tests, black holes and cosmology. Full lecture videos are on OCW. — [MIT OCW 8.962 syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-962-general-relativity-spring-2020/pages/syllabus)
  - Hughes's own page says the lectures were released on OCW in Spring 2020 and that the notes are being revised and typed up ("a little over half"). — [Hughes 8.962 page](https://web.mit.edu/sahughes/www/8.962/index.html)

**Caltech and Cambridge**
- Caltech Ph 236 abc *General Relativity* (2025–26 catalog):
  - Covers gravitational waves, black holes, relativistic stars, causal structure, cosmology and brane worlds.
  - Prerequisite: "mastery of special relativity at the level of Goldstein's Classical Mechanics, or of Jackson's Classical Electrodynamics."
  - Given in alternate years; instructors listed as Chatziioannou and Teukolsky; part c not offered in 2025–26. — [Caltech catalog Ph 236](https://catalog.caltech.edu/current/2025-26/ph-236-abc)
- Cambridge Part III Mathematics (a master's-level fourth year; UK system):
  - Michaelmas term: General Relativity and Cosmology (24 lectures each).
  - Lent term: Black Holes, Field Theory in Cosmology, and others.
  - Easter term: Gauge-Gravity Duality, and Gravitational Waves and Numerical Relativity.
  - Recent GR lecturer: Prof. C. M. Warnick. — [Cambridge DAMTP Part III Relativity & Gravitation page](https://www.maths.cam.ac.uk/postgrad/part-iii/node/90); [Part III lecture list](https://www.maths.cam.ac.uk/undergrad/files/lecturelist/current/Part_III.pdf)

**Typical 4-year plans**
- Sample physics BS plans place upper-level electives and a capstone in years 3–4. Truman State puts a special relativity course in year 2. Drury notes that upper-level physics courses are offered only every other year, so a desired elective may come around once per two years. — [Seattle U BS plan](https://www.seattleu.edu/academics/all-programs/physics-ba-bs/physics-bs-four-year-plan); [Truman State sample plans](https://www.truman.edu/majors-programs/majors-minors/physics-major/sample-4-year-plans/); [Drury plan PDF](https://www.drury.edu/wp-content/uploads/files/academic-affairs/pdf/graduation_plans/Physics.pdf)

### Inferences
- Typical US sequence for a GR-bound physics major. This is synthesized from the prerequisites above and should be presented as "typical," not universal.
  - **Year 1:** calculus-based mechanics and E&M; Calculus I–II.
  - **Year 2:** modern physics (includes special relativity); Calculus III (multivariable); differential equations; linear algebra; often a math-methods course.
  - **Year 3:** classical mechanics (Lagrangian/Hamiltonian); E&M (Griffiths level); quantum mechanics I; thermal/statistical physics; possibly computational physics.
  - **Year 4:** QM II, advanced lab, senior thesis/research, and electives such as GR (Hartle/Schutz), astrophysics and cosmology.
  - **Optional math:** differential geometry, real analysis, PDEs. Most useful for students heading toward mathematical relativity.
- For an engineering student who has done intro physics and is in Calc II:
  - Most intro physics, the calculus sequence, differential equations and linear algebra likely transfer.
  - The main new courses are modern physics, intermediate classical mechanics, E&M II-level work, QM, and stat mech.
  - The GR elective is realistically 2–3 years out after switching.
- Because "only a handful" of departments offered undergraduate GR in Hartle's informal survey, and many departments offer upper electives only in alternate years, the reader should check whether their school actually offers GR. If it doesn't, the routes are reading course or independent study, or self-study from Hartle/Schutz with MIT OCW.

## 2. Graduate study: first-year courses, GR texts, qualifying exams, choosing an advisor, time to PhD, subfields

### Takeaway
A US physics PhD usually means:
- one to two years of core coursework (classical mechanics, E&M, QM, stat mech, math methods);
- a qualifying exam on those cores;
- picking an advisor around the end of year one or in year two;
- then research, for a median of about 6 years in total (mean 6.2).

Recent US physics PhDs had a median age at doctorate of about 29.6. GR is one specialty among many. Its graduate texts are Carroll (the standard first course), Wald (rigorous), MTW (encyclopedic) and Poisson (advanced toolkit).

### Cited Findings
**Courses and qualifying exams**
- First-year core and qualifiers vary by school. Examples:
  - **UC Santa Cruz:** five written tests (math methods, classical mechanics, QM, stat mech, E&M) with retakes allowed through the second year. First-year courses can be waived if equivalent graduate courses were taken and the qualifier is passed.
  - **Purdue:** a two-part, three-hour-each exam given twice a year and taken on entry. — [UCSC Physics PhD catalog](https://catalog.ucsc.edu/en/2019-2020/general-catalog/academic-units/physical-and-biological-sciences-division/physics/graduate-program/physics-phd); [Purdue Graduate Manual 2012](https://www.physics.purdue.edu/academic-programs/docs/Graduate%20Resources/Graduate_Manual_2012.pdf) (older; verify)

**Choosing an advisor**
- **Stanford:** a program advisor guides course choices and rotations until the student joins a research group, "usually near the end of the first year." — [Stanford Physics advising](https://physics.stanford.edu/graduate/graduate-resources/advising)
- **Yale:**
  - Start exploring advisors in year 1; formal affiliation often comes in the 3rd–4th term, after the qualifier.
  - The department does not assign mentors.
  - Check that the advisor has funding and space. — [Yale Physics "Choosing an adviser"](https://physics.yale.edu/academics/graduate-studies/graduate-student-handbook/choosing-adviser)
- **William & Mary:**
  - Students are expected to identify an advisor by spring break of year 1.
  - The summer after year 1 is split between about 8 weeks of research and 5 weeks of qualifier prep.
  - Some advisors may already be full. — [W&M first-year summer program](https://wm.edu/as/physics/grad/the-phd/firstyearsummerprogram)

**Admissions: the Physics GRE (2026 cycle)**
- Not used or not accepted: Stony Brook, Arizona State, New Mexico Tech, Tufts. — [Stony Brook](https://www.stonybrook.edu/physics-new/graduate/apply.html); [ASU](https://phy.asu.edu/admissions/phd-admissions)
- Optional (UCLA, 2026–27): voluntary scores are reviewed holistically. — [UCLA](https://www.pa.ucla.edu/graduate-students.html)
- Not required but the Physics subject test is "strongly recommended": Caltech (Fall 2026) and Stanford. — [Caltech](https://pma.caltech.edu/research-and-academics/physics/physics-graduate-studies/information-for-applicants-2); [Stanford FAQ](https://physics.stanford.edu/prospective-students/graduate-admissions/application-faqs)

**Time to degree, age, cohort size**
- Time to PhD: mean 6.2 years and median 6 years (combined classes of 2017 and 2018).
- 1,903 physics PhDs were conferred by 204 US departments in 2018–19.
- 46% of the class of 2019 were non-US citizens. — [AIP, Trends in Physics PhDs (Feb 2021)](https://www.aip.org/statistics/trends-in-physics-phds)
- AIP posted an updated "Years of Graduate Study to Earn a Physics Doctorate" graphic in Feb 2025, but the values are only in an image. — [AIP graphic page](https://www.aip.org/statistics/physics-trends-years-of-graduate-study-to-earn-a-physics-doctorate21-22)
- Median age at doctorate in physics: 29.6–29.7 years in NSF Survey of Earned Doctorates tables for 2022–2024. All physical sciences: about 29.3. — [NSF NCSES SED table (nsf25349)](https://ncses.nsf.gov/pubs/nsf25349/assets/data-tables/tables/nsf25349-tab008-011.pdf); [NSF NCSES SED table (nsf24336)](https://ncses.nsf.gov/pubs/nsf24336/assets/data-tables/tables/nsf24336-tab008-011.pdf)

**Graduate GR textbooks (verified)**
- **Carroll, *Spacetime and Geometry*:**
  - The Cambridge University Press 2019 edition (ISBN 9781108488396) is a reissue/reprint of the earlier text, not a revision.
  - Covers black holes, perturbation theory and gravitational waves, and cosmology.
  - Required text in MIT 8.962. — [Cambridge front matter](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811084/88396/frontmatter/9781108488396_frontmatter.pdf); [library record](https://bemis.marmot.org/Record/.b65102526); [MIT 8.962 syllabus](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-962-general-relativity-spring-2020/pages/syllabus)
- **Carroll, *Lecture Notes on General Relativity* (free):**
  - arXiv gr-qc/9712019, 238 pages, submitted 3 Dec 1997.
  - A lightly edited version of his MIT 8.962 notes (Spring 1996), aimed at beginning graduate students. — [arXiv gr-qc/9712019](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/9712019)
- **Wald, *General Relativity*:** University of Chicago Press, 1984, about 491 pp. A "mathematically rigorous" introduction. — [Wikipedia: General Relativity (book)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Relativity_(book))
- **Misner, Thorne & Wheeler, *Gravitation* (MTW):**
  - First published by W. H. Freeman in 1973.
  - Princeton University Press reissued it in 2017 with a new foreword by David Kaiser and a new preface by Misner and Thorne (ISBN 978-0-691-17779-3). — [UCLouvain review record](https://ojs.uclouvain.be/index.php/Questions-scientifiques/article/view/70403); [Princeton Alumni Weekly](https://paw.princeton.edu/new-books/gravitation)
- **Poisson, *A Relativist's Toolkit: The Mathematics of Black-Hole Mechanics*:**
  - Cambridge University Press, 2004.
  - Covers geodesic congruences, hypersurfaces, and Lagrangian/Hamiltonian GR.
  - "Not meant to serve as an introduction to general relativity." — [Wikipedia: Eric Poisson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eric_Poisson)

**Subfields and how they are organized** (examples from research-group pages)
- **Numerical relativity:**
  - Cornell's SXS group simulates binary black hole and neutron star mergers. It uses SpEC in production and is developing SpECTRE as the successor. — [Cornell, Nils Deppe group](https://physics.cornell.edu/nils-deppe)
  - QMUL projects use GRChombo on HPC; "C++ coding and use of HPC will be a key part of any projects." — [QMUL, Katy Clough PhD projects](https://qmul.ac.uk/maths/postgraduate/postgraduate-research/phd-projects/current-phd-projects/numerical-relativity-for-fundamental-physics)
- **Mathematical relativity:**
  - The black hole stability conjecture has been called "one of the most important open problems in General Relativity" (Siyuan Ma, Otto Hahn Medal for his dissertation). — [AEI news](https://www.aei.mpg.de/234242/otto-hahn-medal-for-siyuan-ma)
  - A December 2025 Chalmers PhD posting covers black hole stability, uniqueness, and decay of fields. — [Hyperspace listing](https://hyperspace.uni-frankfurt.de/2025/12/22/phd-position-in-mathematical-general-relativity/)
- **Pulsar timing:** NANOGrav's 15-year data set (June 2023) found evidence for a nanohertz gravitational-wave background using 67 pulsars, with Hellings–Downs correlations. — [arXiv:2306.16213](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16213)
- **Precision tests with atomic clocks:** JILA (Bothwell, Ye et al., *Nature* 602, 420, 2022) resolved the gravitational redshift across a millimetre-scale strontium atom cloud, at fractional frequency uncertainty 7.6×10⁻²¹. — [JILA reference](https://jila.colorado.edu/bibcite/reference/12594); [Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/a/gravitational-time-dilation-measured-on-centimetre-and-millimetre-scales-in-atomic-clocks)
- **Black hole imaging (EHT):** Data are correlated at MIT Haystack and MPIfR Bonn with the DiFX software correlator. Imaging is an inference problem because the array is sparse. — [EHT Paper II, arXiv:1906.11239](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.11239); [BHPIRE VLBI imaging explainer](https://bhpire.arizona.edu/2020/03/24/vlbi-data-series-3-vlbi-imaging-techniques)

### Inferences
- GR subfields, roughly ordered from most mathematical to most hands-on. For each, where it usually lives and how students get in:
  1. **Mathematical relativity** (PDE/geometric analysis): often in math departments; needs real analysis and differential geometry; recruits via math PhDs.
  2. **Quantum gravity / high-energy theory:** physics theory groups; needs QFT on top of GR; very competitive.
  3. **Analytic GR / black hole perturbation theory / post-Newtonian theory:** physics theory; heavy use of Mathematica.
  4. **Numerical relativity:** physics or astro; C++/Fortran plus HPC.
  5. **Cosmology:** theory, simulation or data.
  6. **Gravitational-wave data analysis:** Python, Bayesian statistics, large collaborations (LVK).
  7. **Pulsar timing:** radio astronomy plus statistics (NANOGrav).
  8. **Experimental:** LIGO instrumentation, optics, atomic clocks, lab tests of gravity.
  9. **Observational:** EHT/VLBI, X-ray and radio observations of compact objects.
- Students in the US usually apply to a department, not to an advisor, and choose a group in year 1–2. This differs in the UK and Europe (see Gaps), where PhD applications are often to a specific project or supervisor.
- For the reader: a 26-year-old switching now might finish a BS around 28–29 and a PhD around 34–36. That is older than the 29.6 median at doctorate, but within the normal spread.

## 3. What day-to-day research looks like in each mode (theory, computation, data analysis, experiment, observation)

### Takeaway
Across modes, most days are not breakthroughs:
- **Theory:** long blocks of calculation (pen and paper plus Mathematica/xAct), reading arXiv, and being stuck a lot.
- **Computation:** C++/Python code on clusters, debugging and convergence testing.
- **GW data analysis:** Python (GWpy, PyCBC, Bilby), Bayesian inference, plots, and collaboration telecons.
- **Experiment:** hands-on detector or optics work, commissioning, and sometimes observatory shifts.

### Cited Findings
**Theory (analytical)**
- Interviews with 11 theoretical physics faculty (Verostek, Griston, Botello & Zwickl; arXiv 2304.13684, *The Physics Teacher* draft, 2023) found:
  - All agreed the foremost requirement is "blocks of uninterrupted time." One protects three afternoon hours to "read papers thoroughly and do mathematical calculations more carefully."
  - Days are a variable mix of research, teaching and administration: "There is no typical day in my career."
  - On difficulty: "If you were to ask me on a typical day, I don't think I am particularly good at what I do, because most days you are stuck and you're struggling with something."
  - Perseverance is valued over brilliance. One theorist spent 1995–1998 on an approach that failed: "My entire professional life from 1995 until 1998 is a footnote in chapter five of my doctoral dissertation."
  - On expectations: "Some students think they like theory because they have seen some science fiction movies… they don't really know what is involved."
  - On collaboration: "Even my students who are working with me, I consider them to be my collaborators."
  - On tools and styles: "Somebody else who might not even like pen and paper work like I do, but is very good with computers, would make a substantial contribution in a numerical computing project." — [Verostek et al., arXiv:2304.13684](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13684)
- xAct is a free, open-source (GPL) suite of Mathematica packages for tensor algebra, "used and trusted by many researchers in theoretical physics." Its xTensor and xCoba packages compute Christoffel symbols, curvature tensors and geodesic equations. — [NTUA xAct for GR page](https://het.physics.ntua.gr/konstant/GR/xAct.html)
- A theoretical physics postdoc's profile (weak signal): the day starts with email and "reading the latest scientific articles online," then a desk job of research, seminars and video calls. — [I'm a Scientist profile (Vescovi)](https://subject.imascientist.org.uk/profile/edoardovescovi)

**Computational (numerical relativity)**
- **Cornell SXS group:** solves "Einstein's equations of general relativity using massively parallel computer simulations." SpEC is used for production; SpECTRE is the "next-generation multi-physics code." — [Cornell Deppe group](https://physics.cornell.edu/nils-deppe)
- SpECTRE combines a discontinuous Galerkin method with task-based parallelism. First complete SpECTRE binary black hole simulations (inspiral–merger–ringdown) were reported at APS 2025 (arXiv:2410.00265). — [APS April 2025 abstract](https://archive.aps.org/smt/2025/apr-p17/5)
- **UBC (Choptuik group):**
  - Methods: finite-difference and spectral methods for nonlinear PDEs, HPC on distributed-memory clusters, custom visualization, and even compiler design for special-purpose NR languages.
  - Students get a workstation and Compute Canada supercomputer access. — [UBC NR group opportunities](https://bh0.physics.ubc.ca/Group/Opportunities.html)
- **QMUL (GRChombo):** HPC solution of "coupled non-linear partial differential equations of general relativity"; C++ and HPC are central. — [QMUL Clough projects](https://qmul.ac.uk/maths/postgraduate/postgraduate-research/phd-projects/current-phd-projects/numerical-relativity-for-fundamental-physics)

**Gravitational-wave data analysis**
- **Bilby:** a Python library for gravitational-wave parameter estimation, designed to be usable by beginners and also to work on real LIGO/Virgo data (ApJS 2019). — [Bilby paper, arXiv:1811.02042](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/1811.02042)
- **PyCBC:** Bayesian inference modules for compact-binary signals ([arXiv:1807.10312](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.10312)). Official notebooks cover data access, signal processing, matched filtering, significance testing, and an inference track. — [gwastro/PyCBC-Tutorials](https://github.com/gwastro/PyCBC-Tutorials)
- **GWpy quickstart:** fetches public GW150914 data from LIGO Livingston, then bandpasses, whitens and plots it. — [GWpy quickstart](https://gwpy.readthedocs.io/en/latest/quickstart/)
- **A LIGO PhD student's day** (Glasgow, also worked at Hanford; weak signal, personal profile, about 2019):
  - About an hour on email and papers, then coding or writing, and sometimes making posters.
  - At Hanford: "I work on a number of things related to the detector itself."
  - Research: searches for gravitational waves after a kilonova is seen first. — [I'm a Scientist profile (Datrier)](https://spacej19.imascientist.org.uk/profile/laurencedatrier)
- At LIGO Livingston, data-analysis grad student Marissa Walker described her work as "poring over plots and graphs," with constant email and teleconferences with other sites. — [LSU Reveille, Feb 29 2016](https://lsureveille.com/220429/news/lsu-students-alumni-involved-in-ligo-reflect-on-discovery)

**Experimental (detector and instrumentation)**
- **LSU Reveille, Feb 29 2016, on LIGO Livingston:**
  - The site cycles through three modes: installation, commissioning and data collection.
  - Grad student Terra Hardwick spent mornings "running cables, measuring sensor placements or blasting music on loudspeakers" (testing acoustic coupling) and afternoons plotting data.
  - Commissioner Anamaria Effler: "you go into the lab when you feel like it, but you can't choose when you leave." — [LSU Reveille](https://lsureveille.com/220429/news/lsu-students-alumni-involved-in-ligo-reflect-on-discovery)
- **APS News (2008), "Astrowatch":** grad students staffed LIGO Hanford evening and night shifts, two per shift, while also doing daytime maintenance and keeping up with their own research. — [APS News 2008](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2008/08/astrowatch-keeps-ligos-eyes-on-the-sky)
- LIGO Magazine issue 6 (March 2015) has a feature "Detector Commissioning: Control Room Days and Nights" (full text not reviewed). — [LIGO Magazine #6 PDF](https://ligo.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/LIGO-magazine-issue-6.pdf)
- **LIGO job postings show the work content:**
  - **Research scientist:** improving "sensing and control systems that govern optical cavity lengths and angles," both servo stability and noise performance. — [Caltech research scientist posting](https://jobs.weekday.works/caltech-research-scientist-9qyn)
  - **Operations specialist:** 24-hour monitoring of interferometer systems; learn optics, electro-mechanics, electronic controls and DAQ on the job; "after-hours and weekend work is occasionally required to support commissioning." — [Operations Specialist posting](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/unijobs/listing/231084/operations-specialist/)
  - **Electronics engineer:** design and test "ultra-precision instrumentation and control systems." — [Electronics Engineer posting (2020)](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/unijobs/en-au/listing/223197/electronics-engineer/)
- **Atomic-clock tests of GR:** JILA's 2022 millimetre-scale gravitational redshift measurement is an example of tabletop precision-measurement GR work. — [JILA](https://jila.colorado.edu/bibcite/reference/12594)

**Observational (radio/VLBI)**
- **EHT:** correlation at Haystack and MPIfR (DiFX), then imaging as statistical inference. — [arXiv:1906.11239](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.11239); [BHPIRE](https://bhpire.arizona.edu/2020/03/24/vlbi-data-series-3-vlbi-imaging-techniques)
- A recent funded PhD posting (IAA-CSIC Granada + CfA) on dynamic and multi-frequency VLBI imaging for the next-generation EHT (ngEHT). — [AAS Job Register ad](https://aas.org/jobregister/ad/62adf163)

**Detector status context (2026)**

## 7. Learning pathway from Calculus II to graduate GR (ordering, timeframes, self-study)

### Takeaway
Order of study:
1. Calculus III, linear algebra and differential equations.
2. Special relativity (Taylor & Wheeler, free) and intermediate mechanics.
3. A physics-first GR course (Hartle, or Exploring Black Holes, free).
4. Tensors and differential geometry (eigenchris videos, then Carroll's notes or Schutz).
5. Graduate GR (Carroll, or MIT 8.962 on OCW).

Full-time students typically reach undergraduate GR in years 3–4 of a physics major and graduate GR in year 1–2 of a PhD. Self-study can run in parallel with coursework.

### Cited Findings
- **Hartle-level GR needs only intermediate mechanics and SR.** Tensor calculus is not required at that level because only the metric is used. — [Hartle 2005](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/gr-qc/0506075)
- **Prerequisites at each level:**
  - MIT 8.033 (relativity): multivariable calculus and E&M. — [MIT 8.033 F24](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-033-introduction-to-relativity-and-spacetime-physics-fall-2024/pages/syllabus)
  - MIT 8.286 (cosmology): differential equations and E&M. — [MIT 8.286](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-286-the-early-universe-fall-2013/pages/syllabus/)
  - MIT 8.962 (graduate GR): differential equations, linear algebra and E&M II. — [MIT 8.962](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-962-general-relativity-spring-2020/pages/syllabus)
  - Caltech Ph 236: SR mastery at the Goldstein/Jackson level. — [Caltech Ph 236](https://catalog.caltech.edu/current/2025-26/ph-236-abc)
- **Schutz First Course** assumes SR, vector calculus and electrostatics. — [Iberlibro listing](https://www.iberlibro.com/9780521277037/First-Course-General-Relativity-Bernard-0521277035/plp)
- **Susskind & Cabannes, *General Relativity: The Theoretical Minimum*:**
  - Starts from the equivalence principle.
  - Teaches the needed Riemannian geometry and tensor calculus, then black holes, the Einstein field equations and gravitational waves, in 10 "lectures." — [Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9781541601772)
- **Self-study video courses:**
  - Susskind's Stanford Continuing Studies GR course (Fall 2012 run listed on theoreticalminimum.com) is the 4th of 6 core courses. Carroll called it "a fairly standard advanced-undergrad or beginning-grad introduction." — [theoreticalminimum.com courses](https://theoreticalminimum.com/courses); [Discover / Carroll blog 2009](https://www.discovermagazine.com/the-sciences/susskind-lectures-on-general-relativity)
  - A 2026 KIAS "leisurely" nine-month online GR course "for physics students of all ranks" posts recordings to YouTube (@well-tempered) after the series ends. — [KIAS GR2026](https://events.kias.re.kr/h/GR2026/)

### Inferences
Suggested pathway with approximate times (synthesis; times assume a full-time student, or about 5–10 hrs/week of self-study alongside courses):

**Phase 0 — now (Calc II semester), 0–4 months**
- Conceptual reading: Thorne, Epstein, Einstein Online, PBS Space Time.
- Start Taylor & Wheeler *Spacetime Physics*, chapters 1–3 (algebra-level SR).

**Phase 1 — next 2–3 semesters**
- Coursework: Calculus III (multivariable/vector calculus), linear algebra, ODEs; modern physics (SR, QM intro).
- Self-study:
  - Finish *Spacetime Physics*.
  - Start *Exploring Black Holes* 2e (free; needs only calculus) or Schutz *Gravity from the Ground Up*.
  - Watch eigenchris "Tensors for Beginners."
  - Learn Python. Do the GWpy and GWOSC tutorials and a geodesic integrator.

**Phase 2 — years 2–3 of the major**
- Coursework: intermediate classical mechanics (Lagrangian/Hamiltonian), E&M, math methods, QM, stat mech.
- Self-study: Hartle *Gravity* or Schutz *First Course* (with the GR elective if offered); MIT 8.286 OCW for cosmology; Susskind's GR book/lectures; eigenchris "Tensor Calculus" and "Relativity."
- Apply for REUs, including LIGO SURF.

**Phase 3 — senior year**
- Carroll's free lecture notes and the MIT 8.962 videos (Hughes).
- A senior thesis in GR/GW/cosmology.
- Optional: a differential geometry course.
- Apply to grad school.

**Phase 4 — grad school**
- Carroll *Spacetime and Geometry*, then Wald / MTW / Poisson as the specialty requires.
- Choose a subfield and group by the end of year 1 or in year 2.

**Notes on the pathway**
- A realistic calendar for the reader:
  - About 2.5–3 years from now to a solid undergraduate GR course (Hartle/Schutz level).
  - About 3.5–4 years to a bachelor's.
  - About 5–6 more years for a PhD.
- Self-study can get the reader to Hartle/Exploring Black Holes level in about a year alongside coursework. Reaching graduate level (Carroll) realistically needs linear algebra, differential equations and some E&M/mechanics maturity first.
- Risk to flag: jumping straight into tensor-heavy texts (Carroll, Wald, MTW) at Calc II level is a common cause of frustration. Physics-first texts were designed to avoid this.

### Gaps
- No empirical data on how long self-learners take to reach each stage. All timeframes above are reasoned estimates and should be presented as such.
- Non-US pathways (e.g., UK three-year BSc plus a Part III/MSc-style year) are noted only through the Cambridge Part III example.

## 9. Good questions a student could ask a GR professor (about their research and the field)

### Takeaway
Professors and advising guides respond best to specific questions, and the published lists focus on logistics, group culture and fit. A student should:
- read one or two of the professor's recent papers or abstracts first;
- ask about the problem, methods and skills needed;
- ask how undergraduates or new students can contribute;
- ask about group logistics and career outcomes;
- prefer questions that show curiosity about their specific work over generic "what is GR" questions.

### Cited Findings
**Quantum Frontiers / IQIM (Evan Miyazono, 2014), "Top 10 questions for your potential PhD adviser/group"** — [Quantum Frontiers](https://quantumfrontiers.com/2014/05/25/top-10-questions-for-your-potential-phd-advisergroup)
- *For the advisor:*
  - Are you taking new students?
  - What is the procedure for joining the group?
  - Would you have me TA? (Experimental: a polite way to ask about RA funding. Theory: ask how student funding normally works.)
  - Do new students report to grad students, postdocs or the PI?
  - How do you prefer to arrange meetings?
  - How often does the group meet, and how much should students prepare?
  - Should I take any specific classes?
  - What makes someone a good fit for this group?
- *For current group members* (ask more than one):
  - How much do you prepare for meetings with the PI?
  - How long until people lead their own projects?
  - Is the PI hands-on? Accessible?
  - What is the average time to graduation?
  - Do you think I should join this group?
  - What are people's backgrounds?
- **University of Illinois PHYS 596 "Finding an Advisor" slides (2020):**
  - Ask whether projects are collaborative or individual, and whether funding is steady or includes periodic TA duties.
  - Attend seminars, and talk to current students about group culture. — [Illinois slides](https://courses.physics.illinois.edu/phys596/fa2020/Lectures/FindingAdvisorFa20.pdf)
- **Optica, "Selecting an Advisor: Twenty-Five Questions to Ask"** (by Harvard physics grad students) exists, but the full text is paywalled. — [Optica OPN](https://www.optica-opn.org/home/articles/volume_11/issue_4/departments/selecting_an_advisor_twenty-five_questions_to_ask/)
- **UCSD:** check faculty research pages and where former students ended up; group websites usually list current and past students. — [UCSD Physics grad tips](https://physics.ucsd.edu/node/117)
- **Forum advice** (weak signal):
  - Read up on the professor's problem first and ask follow-up questions about what you read.
  - Ask which papers they'd recommend; this can reveal whether they run simulations you could help with (programming or data analysis).
  - Keep the first email short: who you are, your year, what you're looking for. — [Physics Forums: approaching professors](https://www.physicsforums.com/threads/tips-for-approaching-professors-for-undergraduate-research-opportunities.367393/)
- **Bertschinger (MIT):** "Find a topic that inspires you, read all you can about it, and keep asking questions." Also: "Find a community of like-minded people." — [Bertschinger FAQ](https://web.mit.edu/edbert/Students_HS.html)
- **Theory faculty** say students misjudge their work as glamorous and genius-driven. Questions that show awareness of the real process (being stuck, perseverance, collaboration) align with what theorists want students to understand. — [Verostek et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13684)

### Inferences
Suggested GR-specific questions (my synthesis, not from a single source; adapt to the professor's subfield).

**About their research**
- "What's the central open question your group is working on right now, and why does it matter for GR?"
- "In your recent paper on [X], what was the hardest step? Was it a calculation, a numerical issue, or a data problem?"
- "What does a typical week look like for your grad students: derivations, coding, data, lab time?"
- "Which tools do you use most (Mathematica/xAct, Python/Bilby/PyCBC, C++/SpECTRE/Einstein Toolkit, lab hardware)?"
- "What result would surprise you most in the next five years (from LVK O5, LISA, pulsar timing, EHT)?"

**About getting involved**
- "What could an undergraduate realistically contribute to your group, and what should I learn first?"
- "Is there a small self-contained project, such as reproducing a figure from one of your papers, that I could try?"
- "Which courses matter most for your area: differential geometry, numerical methods, statistics, QFT?"
- "Do you take students through REUs or summer programs, or only during the semester?"
- "Would my engineering background (controls, signal processing, CAD, electronics) be useful in your work?"

**About the field and careers**
- "How do you see the GR job market? Where have your recent students and postdocs gone?"
- "Which subfields of gravitational physics are growing, and which are crowded?"
- "If you were starting a PhD today, what would you work on?"
- "What do students most often misunderstand about research in your area?"

**How to ask well**
- Read the abstract and introduction of one or two recent papers first; check their group page for past students.
- Keep the first email short and specific.
- Ask current grad students about group culture separately.
