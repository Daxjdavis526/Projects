## PHYS 3550 Intermediate Classical Mechanics: key questions (1)–(11)

### Takeaway
USU's catalog describes PHYS 3550 as Newtonian mechanics: single- and multi-particle dynamics, conservation laws, oscillations, central forces, rotations and the two-body problem. It says Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics, accelerated frames and chaos "may also be included". Torre's offerings taught it from Taylor's *Classical Mechanics*, with fluency in the Newtonian, Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations as the stated goal. The Atlas should use Taylor Part I (ch. 1–11) plus ch. 12 (chaos) and ch. 13 (Hamiltonian) as core. Poisson brackets, canonical transformations, Hamilton–Jacobi theory, action-angle variables, scattering and continuum/field Lagrangians are enrichment, and several of them become core on the GR track.

#### (3) Module → submodule → subtopic tree

**M0 Newtonian toolkit and coordinate systems [C]** — Taylor ch. 1–2 (†); T&M ch. 1–2 (†)
- 0.1 Inertial frames and Newton's laws. The third law in weak versus strong form (the strong form is needed for conservation of angular momentum).
- 0.2 Cartesian, plane-polar, cylindrical and spherical coordinates.
  - Rotating unit vectors: dr̂/dt = φ̇φ̂ and dφ̂/dt = −φ̇r̂.
  - Polar acceleration: a = (r̈ − rφ̇²)r̂ + (rφ̈ + 2ṙφ̇)φ̂.
- 0.3 Velocity-dependent forces.
  - Linear drag f = bv versus quadratic drag f = cv². The ratio f_quad/f_lin grows with Dv (a Reynolds-type criterion).
  - Terminal speed; projectile with linear drag.
- 0.4 Charged particle in a uniform B. The complex variable η = vx + i·vy solves it; cyclotron frequency ω = qB/m. E×B drift [E].
- Archetypes:
  - (a) Range of a projectile with linear drag, expanded to first order in the drag.
  - (b) Vertical fall with quadratic drag (tanh solution); terminal speed of a skydiver or reentry capsule.
  - (c) Small-oscillation period of a skateboard in a half-pipe, done in polar coordinates.
  - (d) Pitch of the helical path of a proton in B.

**M1 Conservation laws [C]** — Taylor ch. 3–4 (†)
- 1.1 Momentum, centre of mass, and the rocket equation Δv = v_ex ln(m₀/m). Multistage rockets (aerospace hook).
- 1.2 Angular momentum of a particle and of a system; torque about the CM; Kepler's second law as conservation of L.
- 1.3 Energy.
  - Work–KE theorem.
  - Conservative forces: ∇×F = 0 and path independence. F = −∇U.
  - Time-dependent U; energy of interacting particles.
  - 1D energy diagrams and turning points. Period T = 2∫dx/√[(2/m)(E − U)].
- 1.4 One-dimensional curvilinear systems: a bead on a wire, a cube balanced on a cylinder (†).
- Archetypes:
  - Burnout speed of a rocket rising in uniform gravity.
  - A curl test to decide whether a force is conservative.
  - Period of an x⁴ oscillator from the energy integral.
  - Stability of an equilibrium from the sign of U″.

**M2 Oscillations [C]** — Taylor ch. 5 (†); T&M ch. 3 (†)
- 2.1 Simple harmonic motion and complex exponentials; energy; the 2D oscillator and Lissajous figures.
- 2.2 Damping, with β = b/2m.
  - Underdamped (ω₁ = √(ω₀² − β²)), critically damped and overdamped cases.
  - Energy decay.
- 2.3 Driven damped oscillator.
  - Amplitude A² = f₀²/[(ω₀² − ω²)² + 4β²ω²]; phase δ = arctan[2βω/(ω₀² − ω²)].
  - Resonance; Q = ω₀/2β; full width ≈ 2β; transients.
- 2.4 Fourier series for periodic driving; superposition; RMS response via Parseval [E].
- Archetypes:
  - Q from a ring-down trace.
  - Critical damping in an accelerometer or suspension design (aerospace hook).
  - Square-wave driving via Fourier series.
  - The 90° phase lag at resonance.

**M3 Calculus of variations [C]** — Taylor ch. 6 (†); T&M ch. 6 (†)
- 3.1 Functionals. Vary the path as y + αη, then derive the Euler–Lagrange (EL) equation ∂f/∂y − d/dx(∂f/∂y′) = 0. (Derivation walkthrough.)
- 3.2 Classic problems.
  - Geodesics: the shortest path in the plane; great circles on a sphere; geodesics on a cylinder or cone.
  - Brachistochrone (a cycloid); minimal surface of revolution (a catenoid).
  - Fermat's principle giving Snell's law (cross-link to Optics O3).
- 3.3 First integral when f has no explicit x (Beltrami identity: f − y′∂f/∂y′ = const). Choosing the independent variable wisely.
- 3.4 Several dependent variables; isoperimetric constraints with Lagrange multipliers, e.g. the hanging chain [E].
- Archetypes:
  - The brachistochrone.
  - A geodesic on a cone, found by unrolling and by EL, with the two compared.
  - Snell's law from minimum travel time.
  - Identify-the-error: a sign slip in the d/dx term of the EL equation.

**M4 Lagrangian mechanics [C]** — Taylor ch. 7 (†); T&M ch. 7 (†); G ch. 1–2 (†)
- 4.1 Hamilton's principle: S = ∫L dt with L = T − U. The action is *stationary*, not necessarily minimal.
- 4.2 Generalized coordinates, degrees of freedom and configuration space.
  - Holonomic constraints.
  - Time-independent versus time-dependent constraints.
  - Nonholonomic constraints, e.g. a rolling disk [E].
- 4.3 Lagrange's equations.
  - Generalized force ∂L/∂qᵢ and generalized momentum pᵢ = ∂L/∂q̇ᵢ (e.g. p_φ = mr²φ̇).
  - Ignorable (cyclic) coordinates.
- 4.4 Constraint forces via Lagrange multipliers: the normal force on a bead, string tension.
- 4.5 Worked systems.
  - Atwood and double-Atwood machines.
  - Bead on a rotating hoop.
  - Pendulum with a moving support.
  - Spherical pendulum; particle on a cone.
  - Double-pendulum equations of motion.
  - Charged particle with L = ½mv² − qφ + q v·A [E★: gauge structure].
- 4.6 The energy function h = Σpᵢq̇ᵢ − L.
  - h is conserved when ∂L/∂t = 0.
  - h equals T + U only for time-independent constraints and velocity-independent U.
- 4.7 Noether's theorem: continuous symmetries ↔ conserved quantities (translation → p, rotation → L, time translation → h). L → L + dF/dt leaves the equations unchanged [E★].
- Archetypes:
  - Equilibria of the bead on a rotating hoop, and the pitchfork bifurcation at ω² = g/R.
  - Pendulum with a horizontally oscillating support.
  - The normal force from a multiplier, and the angle at which a particle leaves a hemisphere.
  - "Is h conserved? Is h = E?" for a bead on a rotating rod.

**M5 Two-body central forces and orbital mechanics [C]** — Taylor ch. 8 (†); T&M ch. 8 (†); G ch. 3 (†)
- 5.1 CM and relative coordinates; reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂).
- 5.2 Conserved ℓ and planar motion; reduction to an equivalent 1D problem.
  - Effective potential U_eff = U + ℓ²/(2μr²); turning points.
  - Circular orbits and their stability: for F ∝ −rⁿ, stable iff n > −3.
- 5.3 Orbit equation in u = 1/r.
  - Kepler solution r = c/(1 + ε cos φ), with c = ℓ²/(γμ) and E = γ²μ(ε² − 1)/(2ℓ²).
  - Conic classification. (Derivation walkthrough.)
- 5.4 Kepler's laws: τ² = 4π²a³/[G(m₁ + m₂)]; vis-viva v² = G(m₁ + m₂)(2/r − 1/a).
- 5.5 Changes of orbit (aerospace hook): tangential burns, Hohmann transfer, bi-elliptic transfer [E], gravity assists in the planet's frame [E].
- 5.6 Precession of nearly circular orbits: the apsidal angle is π/√(n + 3) for F ∝ −rⁿ. Bertrand's theorem and the Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector [E].
- 5.7 Schwarzschild bridge [E★].
  - Effective potential per unit mass: V_eff = −GM/r + L²/2r² − GML²/(c²r³).
  - Perihelion advance 6πGM/[c²a(1 − e²)] per orbit (about 43″ per century for Mercury).
  - ISCO at r = 6GM/c²; photon sphere at r = 3GM/c².
- Archetypes:
  - Perigee and apogee from E and ℓ.
  - Hohmann transfer from LEO (r = 6,678 km) to GEO (42,164 km): Δv₁ ≈ 2.43 km/s, Δv₂ ≈ 1.47 km/s, total ≈ 3.9 km/s (researcher's calculation).
  - Stability of a circular orbit under a small 1/r³ perturbation.
  - Perihelion speed of a comet from vis-viva.

**M6 Non-inertial frames [C]** — Taylor ch. 9; T&M ch. 10 (†)
- 6.1 Linearly accelerating frames and the inertial force −mA. A pendulum hanging in an accelerating car. Preview of the equivalence principle.
- 6.2 Tides: the difference of the gravitational field across a body; an estimate of tidal-bulge height.
- 6.3 The angular-velocity vector. Rotating-frame derivative: (dQ/dt)_inertial = (dQ/dt)_rot + Ω×Q.
- 6.4 Newton's second law in a rotating frame: m r̈ = F + 2m ṙ×Ω + m(Ω×r)×Ω (Coriolis and centrifugal terms). (Derivation walkthrough.)
- 6.5 Centrifugal effects: effective g, plumb-line deviation, Earth's oblateness.
- 6.6 Coriolis effects.
  - Horizontal deflection and cyclones.
  - Eastward deflection in free fall ≈ (1/3)Ωgt³ cos λ, where λ is latitude.
  - Foucault-pendulum precession rate Ω sin λ.
- 6.7 A puck on a rotating turntable, with its path shown in both frames.
- Archetypes:
  - Eastward deflection of an object dropped 100 m at a given latitude.
  - Foucault period ≈ 23.93 h / sin λ.
  - Coriolis direction for a long-range projectile.
  - Tidal acceleration across an astronaut near a compact object (bridge to "spaghettification").

**M7 Rigid-body rotation [C]** — Taylor ch. 10 "Rotational Motion of Rigid Bodies"; T&M ch. 11 (†); G ch. 4–5 (†)
- 7.1 CM motion plus rotation; fixed-axis rotation. L is not parallel to ω in general.
- 7.2 Inertia tensor: I_xx = Σm(y² + z²), I_xy = −Σm·xy. Parallel-axis theorem in tensor form.
- 7.3 Principal axes as eigenvectors and principal moments as eigenvalues, from det(I − λ1) = 0.
- 7.4 Euler's equations: λ₁ω̇₁ − (λ₂ − λ₃)ω₂ω₃ = Γ₁, and cyclic permutations. (Derivation walkthrough.)
- 7.5 Torque-free motion.
  - Symmetric body: body and space cones; body-frame precession rate |λ₃ − λ₁|ω₃/λ₁; Earth's free wobble.
  - Asymmetric body: the tennis-racket (intermediate-axis) instability.
  - Energy dissipation drives spin to the major axis, as with Explorer 1 (aerospace hook).
- 7.6 Euler angles and the heavy symmetric top.
  - Lagrangian with cyclic φ and ψ; an effective potential in θ.
  - Precession and nutation; slow gyroscopic precession Ω ≈ MgR/(λ₃ω₃) [C-lite].
- Archetypes:
  - Inertia tensor of a cube about a corner, and its principal axes.
  - Free-precession period of a thrown, spinning plate.
  - Spin stability about each axis of a book or satellite.
  - Precession rate of a gyroscope.

**M8 Coupled oscillators and normal modes [C]** — Taylor ch. 11; T&M ch. 12 (†); G ch. 6 (†); cross-links 3750 W2–W4
- 8.1 Two carts and three springs: M q̈ = −K q.
  - Ansatz q = a·e^{iωt} leads to det(K − ω²M) = 0.
  - Normal coordinates.
- 8.2 Weak coupling, beats, and the energy-exchange time.
- 8.3 General Lagrangian method.
  - Expand U to second order and T to quadratic order about equilibrium, giving M and K.
  - Positive-definite K means stable; ω² < 0 means unstable.
- 8.4 Further systems.
  - Double pendulum with equal m and L: ω² = (2 ± √2)g/L.
  - Three coupled pendula.
  - Molecular vibrations such as CO₂ [E]; zero modes from symmetries [E].
- 8.5 Orthogonality of modes with respect to M; completeness; solving the initial-value problem by projection; preview of the continuum limit, which gives the wave equation.
- Archetypes:
  - Normal modes of unequal masses with three springs.
  - Small oscillations of the double pendulum.
  - Beat period of weakly coupled pendula.
  - Guessing mode shapes from symmetry before computing.

**M9 Hamiltonian mechanics [C for 9.1–9.4; E for 9.5–9.8]** — Taylor ch. 13 (†); G ch. 8–10; MIT 8.09
- 9.1 Legendre transform: H(q, p, t) = Σpq̇ − L.
  - Hamilton's equations q̇ = ∂H/∂p, ṗ = −∂H/∂q.
  - dH/dt = ∂H/∂t. (Derivation walkthrough.)
- 9.2 Examples: the 1D oscillator, a central force in polar coordinates, a particle on a cone. Ignorable coordinates; the Routhian [E].
- 9.3 Phase-space orbits, phase portraits and separatrices.
- 9.4 Liouville's theorem: phase-space flow is incompressible. Links to statistical mechanics in PHYS 3700. (Derivation walkthrough.)
- 9.5 Poisson brackets [E★].
  - df/dt = {f, H} + ∂f/∂t; conserved quantities.
  - {q, p} = 1 as a preview of quantum commutators.
- 9.6 Canonical transformations, generating functions and the symplectic condition [E] (Goldstein §9.1–9.7).
- 9.7 Hamilton–Jacobi equation, separation of variables, action-angle variables, the Kepler problem in action-angle form [E★ for GR: the eikonal limit and the Carter constant (†)] (Goldstein §10.1–10.8).
- 9.8 Adiabatic invariants; canonical perturbation theory [E].
- Archetypes:
  - H and Hamilton's equations for a central force.
  - Pendulum phase portrait: libration versus rotation, and the separatrix energy.
  - Liouville's theorem applied to a beam of particles in a harmonic trap.
  - Checking that a transformation is canonical with Poisson brackets [E].
  - The Hamilton–Jacobi method on a solved problem (mirrors 8.09 PS5) [E].

**M10 Nonlinear mechanics and chaos [C, introductory]** — Taylor ch. 12; T&M ch. 4 (†)
- 10.1 Nonlinearity means superposition fails. The driven damped pendulum: φ̈ + 2βφ̇ + ω₀² sin φ = γω₀² cos ωt.
- 10.2 Attractors, period doubling and bifurcation diagrams. Feigenbaum δ ≈ 4.669.
- 10.3 Sensitive dependence on initial conditions; the Lyapunov exponent (Δφ ∝ e^{λt}).
- 10.4 State-space orbits and Poincaré sections.
- 10.5 Logistic map: fixed points and their stability; period doubling at r = 3, then r = 1 + √6 ≈ 3.449, accumulating at r ≈ 3.5699.
- Archetypes:
  - Estimate λ from the slope of log|Δφ| against t.
  - Read the period from a Poincaré section.
  - Fixed points and stability of the logistic map.
  - Classify a pendulum's response as periodic, period-n or chaotic.

**M11 Collisions and scattering [E]** — Taylor ch. 14
- Cross sections.
  - Differential cross section dσ/dΩ = (b/sin θ)|db/dθ|.
  - Hard spheres: σ = π(R₁ + R₂)².
  - Rutherford: dσ/dΩ = [γ/(4E)]²/sin⁴(θ/2).
- Lab and CM frames.
- GR bridge [E★]: the photon-capture cross section of a Schwarzschild black hole is 27πG²M²/c⁴.
- Archetypes: showing that hard-sphere scattering is isotropic; Rutherford counts versus angle.

**M12 Relativistic Lagrangian [E★]** — Taylor ch. 15 (†); G ch. 7 (†)
- S = −mc²∫dτ, so L = −mc²√(1 − v²/c²) − U.
- Four-vectors.
- Bridge to the geodesic principle.

**M13 Continuous systems and field Lagrangians [E★]** — Taylor ch. 16 (†); MIT 8.09 "continuous systems"; G ch. 13 (†)
- String Lagrangian density ℒ = ½μq̇² − ½Tq′².
- The field Euler–Lagrange equation gives the wave equation.
- The stress tensor leads to the idea of a stress-energy tensor.

#### (4) Mathematical skills
- **Vector calculus:** gradient and curl, used for conservative forces.
- **ODEs:** linear constant-coefficient equations, complex exponentials, separable first-order equations.
- **Linear algebra:** eigenvalues and eigenvectors of symmetric matrices (normal modes, the inertia tensor), generalized eigenproblems det(K − ω²M) = 0, positive-definiteness.
- **Expansions:** Taylor and binomial expansions, linearization about equilibrium.
- **Calculus of variations**, the Legendre transform, Lagrange multipliers.
- **Multivariable calculus:** the chain rule, partial versus total time derivatives.
- **Analysis tools:** Fourier series; phase-plane analysis.
- **Numerical integration (for the sims):** Euler, RK4, and symplectic leapfrog/Verlet, which conserves energy long-term. The difference between them is itself a lesson.

#### (5) Prerequisite knowledge
- Calculus-based introductory mechanics (forces, energy, momentum, rotation about a fixed axis).
- Multivariable calculus.
- Differential equations and linear algebra.
- Introductory modern physics. The sibling catalog notes report that PHYS 2710 is required for PHYS 3550; see Gaps.

#### (6) Foundational concepts the catalog text leaves out
- What makes a frame inertial.
- Configuration space versus phase space.
- Generalized coordinates as *any* independent parameters, not only positions.
- Virtual displacements, and why ideal constraint forces drop out of the equations.
- Stationary rather than least action.
- The non-uniqueness of L (adding a total derivative changes nothing).
- The energy function h versus the energy E.
- Linearization and stability.
- Tensors: the inertia tensor is students' first physical tensor and a GR on-ramp.
- Conserved quantities reduce the order of a problem.
- Dimensional analysis and limiting-case checks.
- Integrable versus chaotic systems.

#### (7) Problem-solving techniques
1. Pick coordinates that respect the symmetry.
2. Write T and U, then form L.
3. Spot cyclic coordinates and their conserved momenta.
4. Reduce to a 1D effective-potential problem.
5. Use energy integrals for periods and turning points.
6. Linearize about equilibrium and use a normal-mode ansatz.
7. Use Lagrange multipliers when constraint forces are wanted.
8. Draw phase portraits instead of solving explicitly.
9. Check limits (m₂ → ∞, β → 0, small angles).
10. Integrate numerically when the system is not integrable.

#### (9) Connections to advanced physics and GR
- **Hamilton's principle → geodesics.**
  - A free particle in GR extremizes proper time, τ = ∫√(−g_μν dx^μ dx^ν)/c. Timelike geodesics locally *maximize* τ, which resolves the twin paradox.
  - The Lagrangian ½g_μν ẋ^μ ẋ^ν yields the Christoffel symbols directly from the EL equations, which makes M3–M4 the computational engine of GR. (Standard GR pedagogy, e.g. Carroll's lecture notes, arXiv gr-qc/9712019 (†, not opened this session).)
- **Cyclic coordinates and Noether (M4.7) → Killing vectors.** A conserved E and L along Schwarzschild geodesics reduces the problem to the same effective-potential analysis as M5 (see M5.7).
- **Tidal forces (M6.2) → geodesic deviation and the Riemann tensor. Accelerated frames → the equivalence principle and Rindler observers.** Coriolis-type terms lead to gravitomagnetism and frame dragging.
- **Inertia tensor (M7) → the first tensor students meet.** The mass quadrupole moment drives the GW quadrupole formula.
- **Constraints and Lagrange multipliers (M4.4, M9) → ADM.** In the Hamiltonian formulation of GR the lapse and shift play the role of Lagrange multipliers that enforce the Hamiltonian and momentum constraints — [ADM, arXiv gr-qc/0405109](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/0405109) (the multiplier interpretation is standard; its lapse/shift text was not retrieved).
- **Hamilton–Jacobi (M9.7) → the eikonal equation, geometric optics and null geodesics; separability of Kerr geodesics (the Carter constant) (†).**
- **Normal modes (M8) → quasinormal modes.** QNMs are complex-frequency modes of open, dissipative systems, measured in black-hole ringdowns — [Berti et al., arXiv 0905.2975](https://arxiv.org/abs/0905.2975).
- **Continuous systems (M13) → field theory and the stress-energy tensor.** Chaos (M10) → chaotic geodesics and mixmaster cosmology [E] (†).

#### (11) Misconceptions and error patterns usable as distractors
These come from teaching experience unless cited; no item-level upper-division PER was retrieved.
- **Fictitious forces:**
  - "Centrifugal force" drawn as a real force on an inertial free-body diagram, or centripetal force drawn as an extra force. Distractor: "After the string breaks, centrifugal force pushes the ball radially outward."
  - Coriolis deflection direction wrong. Vertical motion is also deflected east or west, not only horizontal motion. The "toilet-bowl" myth.
- **Lagrangian setup:**
  - L = T + U, or the wrong sign on gravity when the y-axis points down.
  - Assuming every generalized momentum is mv. In fact p_φ = mr²φ̇, and p = mv + qA for a charge.
  - Assuming h always equals E, or that E is conserved whenever h is. On the driven rotating hoop h is conserved but E is not.
  - Assuming constraint forces never do work. Moving constraints do work.
  - Treating "least action" as a guaranteed minimum.
- **Rigid bodies:**
  - Assuming L = Iω with L parallel to ω always.
  - Dropping the minus sign on products of inertia.
  - Misusing the parallel-axis theorem for an axis not through the CM.
- **Two-body problem and orbits:**
  - Using m₁ instead of μ.
  - Kepler's third law with M instead of M + m.
  - Confusing the semi-major axis with the apoapsis distance.
  - "Thrust prograde to catch a craft ahead of you." In fact you slow down, drop to a lower and faster orbit, then rise again.
  - "Every power-law force has stable circular orbits."
- **Normal modes:**
  - "The in-phase mode has the higher frequency." For spring-coupled masses it is the lower one.
  - "Mode frequencies depend on initial amplitude" (false for linear systems).
  - "An arbitrary start excites only one mode."
- **Phase space:** trajectories of an autonomous system drawn crossing.
- **Chaos:** "chaotic means random"; "chaos needs many degrees of freedom" (the driven damped pendulum has 1.5).
- **Small angles:** applying the small-angle approximation at large amplitude; claiming the period never depends on amplitude.

## PHYS 3700 Thermal Physics: key questions (1)–(11)

### Takeaway
Torre's Spring 2015 offering of PHYS 3700 used Schroeder's *An Introduction to Thermal Physics*, chapters 1–7. Its objectives centred on the laws of thermodynamics, entropy, free energies and model systems (the ideal gas, the Einstein solid). Schroeder's three parts (Fundamentals, Thermodynamics, Statistical Mechanics), plus the probability on-ramp in MIT 8.044, define the core: microstates and entropy, temperature as ∂S/∂U, engines, free energies and phase transitions, Boltzmann statistics and partition functions, and quantum statistics including blackbody radiation and BEC. Schroeder ch. 8 (interacting systems: the virial expansion, the Ising model, Monte Carlo), transport and fluctuation theory are enrichment, though Monte Carlo makes an excellent simulation.

- Quasistatic versus reversible processes; state functions versus path quantities.
- Reservoirs.
- Temperature as ∂S/∂U, not "average kinetic energy". Negative temperature.
- Indistinguishability and the 1/N! factor.
- The quantum concentration and the classical-limit criterion; density of states.
- The chemical potential as "escaping tendency".
- Legendre structure shared with mechanics.
- Information-theoretic entropy; the arrow of time.

#### (7) Problem-solving techniques
1. Count Ω, take logs and apply Stirling.
2. Differentiate S to get T, P and μ.
3. "Compute Z, then everything": ⟨E⟩, F, S, C.
4. Sum over modes as an integral over k with a density of states.
5. Tabulate Q, W and ΔU per leg of a cycle.
6. Use the Maxwell-relation trick and Clausius–Clapeyron integration.
7. Work in dimensionless variables (x = ε/kT, t = T/T_c) and check the high- and low-T limits.
8. Run Monte Carlo when analysis fails.

#### (9) Connections to advanced physics and GR
- **Blackbody radiation (T7.4) → Hawking radiation.** Black holes emit a thermal spectrum quantum-mechanically — [Wald review](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/9912119).
  - T_H = ħc³/(8πGMk_B), which is about 6.2×10⁻⁸ K for one solar mass (researcher's calculation).
  - Bekenstein–Hawking entropy S = k_B c³A/(4Għ).
  - Treating E = Mc² gives a *negative* heat capacity C = −8πGk_B M²/(ħc) (researcher's derivation). This is a superb "boss" twist: an evaporating hole gets hotter. Self-gravitating stars share the property.
- **The four laws (T1–T4) → the four laws of black-hole mechanics; the generalized second law; entropy bounds** — [Wald review](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/9912119).
- **Accelerated frames → the Unruh temperature T = ħa/(2πck_B); gravity → the Tolman–Ehrenfest relation T√(−g_tt) = const** (standard results; not sourced this session).
- **Degenerate Fermi gas (T7.3) → white dwarfs and the Chandrasekhar limit; neutron stars**, which need the GR Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff equation of state (†).
- **Photon gas → the radiation-dominated early universe** (ρ ∝ T⁴ ∝ a⁻⁴) and the CMB blackbody; feeds the Friedmann equations.
- **Liouville and ensembles → relativistic kinetic theory [E]. Partition functions → Euclidean path integrals (Gibbons–Hawking) [far enrichment] (†).**

#### (11) Misconceptions and error patterns usable as distractors
- **Heat versus temperature:** treating them as the same, or treating heat as a substance a body "contains"; not distinguishing heat, work and internal energy (Loverude et al.).
- **Adiabatic compression:** explaining the temperature rise with PV = nRT alone, with no work argument (Loverude et al.). Distractor: "T rises because P rises and V falls, so PV/nR goes up" without saying why.
- **Cycles:** treating Q or W as state functions; claiming ΔU ≠ 0 over a cycle; confusing differential and net changes over a cycle (Smith et al. 2015).
- **Entropy:** "the system's entropy must always increase" (it is the universe's, or an isolated system's); "entropy = disorder", which fails for mixing of identical gases and for crystallization driven by heat release.
- **Carnot:** not connecting the Carnot bound to the second law (Smith et al.); computing 1 − T_c/T_h in °C.
- **Temperature and equipartition:** "temperature is always average KE" (fails for spins and negative T); "equipartition always holds" (fails when kT ≪ ħω).
- **Counting:** forgetting degeneracy (the Boltzmann factor gives the probability of a *state*, not of an energy level); forgetting 1/N!, or applying it to distinguishable lattice sites.
- **Partial derivatives:** dropping the held-constant variable, e.g. (∂U/∂T)_P ≠ C_P (researcher's teaching-experience item; the Thompson-group partial-derivative studies were not retrieved).
- **Wien peaks:** assuming the peak λ equals c/(peak f). It does not: the two peaks differ by a factor of about 1.76.
- **Quantum statistics:** "photons have a nonzero μ"; "BEC happens because bosons attract".
- **Throttling and negative T:** "throttling always cools" (the inversion temperature says otherwise); "negative temperature is colder than absolute zero" (it is hotter than any positive T).

#### (11) PER on waves
- Wittmann, Steinberg & Redish studied student understanding of the propagation and superposition of pulses on long, taut strings, and found:
  - Many students believe the way a pulse is produced affects its speed. The principle that wave speed depends on the medium, not on the initial conditions, is a central target.
  - Students treat a wave as a few key points rather than an extended region. Many do not superpose where the pulses' edges overlap, saying waves only superpose where the peaks coincide.
  - The authors attribute these patterns to an "object" model of waves that imports force, energy and collision ideas from particle mechanics.
  - Sources: [Wittmann dissertation ch. 3](https://www.physics.umd.edu/perg/dissertations/Wittmann/3_difficulties_with_wave_physics.pdf); [UMd PERG Wittmann page](https://physics.umd.edu/perg/dissertations/Wittmann)
- The same misconceptions persist among second-year physics majors, though the fraction holding them falls with instruction — [UWO study (De Bruyn group)](https://physics.uwo.ca/~debruyn/pdfs/erin-waves.pdf)

- QNM spectra are non-Hermitian eigenvalue problems, and measuring QNMs in gravitational-wave data constrains black-hole mass and spin — [Berti et al., arXiv 0905.2975](https://arxiv.org/abs/0905.2975)

#### (4) Mathematical skills
- Complex numbers and phasors.
- Linear ODE systems, matrices, eigenvalue problems and diagonalization.
- PDEs: the wave equation, separation of variables, boundary-value problems.
- Fourier series and transforms, orthogonality and completeness, the delta function.
- Vector calculus (grad, div, curl, the integral theorems); the Laplacian in cylindrical and spherical coordinates.
- An introduction to special functions (Bessel functions, spherical harmonics and Legendre polynomials).
- Continuity equations; Taylor expansion in the continuum limit; dimensional analysis.
- Finite-difference schemes, which the sims use (CFL condition v·Δt/Δx ≤ 1 as a teachable moment).

#### (5) Prerequisite knowledge
- PHYS 2710 and MATH 2210, with MATH 2250 concurrently (per the Riffe 2016 syllabus); calculus-based introductory mechanics and E&M.
- The sibling catalog notes add that the degree map requires MATH 2250 (or 2270/2280) before PHYS 3750.
- PHYS 3550's oscillator and normal-mode modules are ideal but not required predecessors.

#### (6) Foundational concepts the catalog text leaves out
- Linearity as a property of the *equation*, which is what licenses superposition.
- Normal modes as eigenvectors, so "diagonalize and you decouple".
- Completeness: every solution is a sum of modes.
- The dispersion relation as the system's fingerprint.
- Boundary conditions quantize allowed k.
- Phase versus group velocity; information travels at the signal or front velocity.
- Medium motion versus pattern motion.
- Energy flux and local conservation laws; impedance.
- Initial data versus boundary data.
- The causal structure of the wave equation: characteristics x ± vt are "light cones".
- The field concept.

#### (7) Problem-solving techniques
1. Use the ansatz e^{i(kx − ωt)} to get the dispersion relation.
2. Separate variables, then let the boundary conditions quantize k.
3. Fourier-decompose the initial data and evolve each mode.
4. Use d'Alembert's characteristics for initial-value problems.
5. Derive local conservation laws by multiplying the field equation by ∂q/∂t.
6. Set up matrix eigenproblems for discrete systems.
7. Check limiting cases (N → ∞, k → 0).
8. Simulate with finite differences.

#### (9) Connections to advanced physics and GR
- **The wave equation (W4–W7) → gravitational waves.** Linearized GR gives □h̄_μν = −16πG T_μν/c⁴. In vacuum, in transverse-traceless gauge, two polarizations (+, ×) propagate at c. W-V12 makes this concrete. (Standard result; the LIGO detection, GW150914, arXiv 1602.03837 (†), was not opened.)
- **Characteristics (W5) → light cones and causal structure.** The d'Alembertian □ is Lorentz-invariant, which links to special relativity and spacetime diagrams.
- **Normal modes (W2–W3) → quasinormal modes.** Ringdown frequencies are complex because the system leaks energy to infinity: non-Hermitian eigenproblems — [Berti et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/0905.2975).
- **Maxwell (W12) → the field-theory template for GR.** The covariant F_μν leads to Einstein's equations; Poynting energy flux leads to the stress-energy tensor T_μν.
- **Fourier analysis (W6) → GW data analysis.** Matched filtering and noise power spectra are done in the frequency domain (†).
- **Spherical harmonics (W8) → multipole expansions of GWs** (spin-weighted harmonics) and CMB anisotropies (†).
- **The dispersion relation → the mass-shell condition.** A massless graviton has ω = ck; Klein–Gordon leads to fields in curved spacetime [E★].
- **Solitons (W13) → nonlinearity.** GR is nonlinear (gravity gravitates), so superposition fails beyond linear order.

#### (10) Depth, rigor, derivation walkthroughs and boss battle
- **Target depth:** a sophomore–junior mathematical-methods course in which the physics motivates the mathematics. Riffe's syllabus explicitly makes skill-building the aim.
- **Derivation walkthroughs:**
  - The continuum limit.
  - d'Alembert's solution.
  - Fourier coefficients from orthogonality.
  - The chain's dispersion relation.
  - The energy continuity equation.
  - Group velocity from beating waves.
  - The probability current.
  - The EM wave equation from Maxwell.
  - Poynting's theorem.
  - Separation of variables in polar coordinates, leading to Bessel's equation.
- **Out of scope:** distribution theory, rigorous convergence of Fourier series, Green's-function machinery beyond a preview.
- **Boss battle, "Chirp":**
  1. Given a GW-like chirp signal, Fourier-analyze it and find its frequency evolution.
  2. Solve for the modes of a circular drum (the detector mirror's drumhead modes, as an analogy).
  3. Derive the EM wave equation and compute the laser power needed for a target radiation pressure.
  4. Analyze a soliton collision.
  5. Identify-the-error: a phase-versus-group-velocity claim of "faster-than-light signalling".

#### (11) Misconceptions and error patterns usable as distractors
- **Wave speed:** "speed depends on how the pulse was made (amplitude, how fast it was shaken)" rather than on the medium (Wittmann et al.).
- **Waves as objects:** pulses "bounce off" each other; superposition happens only where peaks coincide (Wittmann et al.). These persist into the second year (UWO study).
- **Medium versus pattern:** "particles of the medium travel along with the wave".
- **Modes and Fourier:**
  - "Mode frequencies depend on how hard you pluck."
  - "A short pulse has a single frequency."
  - "Fourier series only work for periodic functions" (no extension).
- **Standing waves:** "no energy at the instant of zero displacement"; "nodes carry no energy flux, so no energy is there".
- **Reflection:** the fixed-end and free-end inversion rules swapped.
- **Phase versus group velocity:** "phase velocity > c violates relativity"; "information travels at phase velocity".
- **EM waves:**
  - "E and B are 90° out of phase in a vacuum plane wave."
  - "|B| = |E|" (missing c).
  - "EM waves need a medium."
- **Schrödinger:** "ψ is a physical displacement"; "the particle moves at the phase velocity ħk/2m". The group velocity ħk/m is the particle velocity.
- **Boundary conditions:** "only fixed ends quantize". Periodic and free boundary conditions quantize too.

#### (9) Connections to advanced physics and GR
- **Fermat's principle (O3) → gravitational lensing.**
  - Light follows null geodesics. Image positions, shapes, brightness, caustics and multiplicity are all treated in [Perlick's review](https://www.maths.tcd.ie/EMIS/journals/LRG/Articles/lrr-2004-9/).
  - In the weak field, lensing is modelled with an effective index n ≈ 1 − 2Φ/c². Light deflection is 4GM/(c²b), twice the "Newtonian" value. Other effects: Shapiro delay, Einstein rings, and time-delay cosmography [E] (standard results, not sourced this session).
  - A relativistic Fermat principle exists and is used in research — [Perlick, gr-qc/0508029](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/gr-qc/0508029).
- **The eikonal limit (O4) → null geodesics.** This links back to Hamilton–Jacobi in PHYS 3550 M9.7.
- **Interferometry (O7) → GW detectors.** LIGO-class detectors are Michelson interferometers with Fabry–Pérot arm cavities that measure the strain h = ΔL/L (†; GW150914, arXiv 1602.03837, not opened).
- **Polarization (O6) → GW polarization.**
  - + and × modes. A GW pattern is invariant under a 180° rotation (spin-2), while EM polarization is invariant under 360° (spin-1).
  - Stokes parameters lead to CMB E- and B-mode polarization (†).
- **Fourier optics (O9) and coherence (O10) → the shared signal-processing toolkit of GW data analysis; intensity interferometry; quantum optics.**
- **The Sagnac interferometer (O7) → rotation sensing (fibre-optic gyros, an aerospace hook)** and the relativistic Sagnac effect.

#### (11) Misconceptions and error patterns usable as distractors
- **Imaging (Goldberg & McDermott):**
  - "Covering half the lens removes half the image." In fact the whole image remains, dimmer.
  - "An image exists only where a screen is." "A virtual image can't be photographed." These two are researcher teaching-experience items; only the half-lens result was retrieved from Goldberg & McDermott.
- **Diffraction (Ambrose et al.):**
  - Not knowing when to use geometrical versus physical optics.
  - Mis-explaining single- and double-slit patterns.
  - Photon misconceptions, e.g. "photons travel along wavy paths" or "photons split at slits like particles" (examples are the researcher's).
  - "A wider slit gives a wider pattern." The width is inversely proportional.
  - Swapping slit width and separation roles (envelope versus fringes).
- **Reflection phase:** "reflection always adds π". It does so only for external reflection off a higher index (with polarization caveats near Brewster).
- **Polarizers:** "polarizers only block". The three-polarizer result shows that components get projected.
- **Brewster:** confusing which polarization survives in reflection (s is reflected; p is fully transmitted at θ_B).
- **Refraction:** "frequency changes in a medium". Wavelength changes; frequency does not.
- **Lasers and group velocity:** "lasers are perfectly coherent / monochromatic"; "any two light sources interfere"; "group velocity > c means signalling faster than light".
- **Conventions:** handedness conventions for circular polarization differ between optics and engineering texts. The Atlas must state its convention, or it will generate false "errors".
- **Lensing analog:** "a gravitational lens focuses to a point". It has no single focal point; it forms focal lines and caustics.

## (9) across all four courses: the GR connection spine, inter-course dependencies and sequencing

### Takeaway
The four courses supply nearly every classical idea that general relativity reuses:
- variational principles and constraints (mechanics);
- normal modes, the wave equation, Fourier analysis and Maxwell's equations (waves);
- blackbody statistics and entropy (thermal physics);
- Fermat's principle, interferometry and polarization (optics).

Build the Atlas so that each course ends with an explicit "GR bridge" lesson and its boss battle reuses those bridges. Order the courses 3550, then 3750, then Optics, with 3700 running after 3550 M9.
