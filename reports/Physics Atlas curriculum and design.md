# Charting a physics degree toward Einstein

The Physics Atlas should teach Utah State University's B.S. in Physics (Professional Emphasis) as one prerequisite graph of 22 course maps (an M0 refresher; the 13-credit mathematics block of MATH 1220, 2210, 2270 and 2280, with MATH 2250 as an accepted fork; introductory physics through Electromagnetism II and Quantum Mechanics II; Optics I, which USU numbers PHYS 4650 because no PHYS 5650 exists; two communications-intensive labs; 4 research credits; and CS 1400), and then carry the learner past the degree through seven "Mathematics of Spacetime" tracks and a 37-step Road to Einstein's Field Equations whose only official USU anchor is the elective PHYS 5910/6910 Relativity I. Because this learner has finished Calculus I and Python, is taking Calculus II and Physics I, learns visually and is drawn to black holes, gravitational waves and propulsion, the decisive design choice is to separate routes: an Intuition-Mode black-hole track, linear algebra and the first numerical-methods, Fourier and proof modules can start this semester, while Derivation Mode waits for tensors and differential geometry. The learning engine should keep two currencies apart, with **XP for effort that hints never reduce** and **mastery states that move only on unassisted, spaced and transfer evidence**, estimated by an Elo-style model, scheduled by FSRS-6 and shown as words and evidence checklists rather than percentages. The build fits this repository's rules as one self-contained static folder that vendors KaTeX, math.js, an on-demand MathLive, three.js r185 and Pyodide 314.0.7 in a worker, keeps a DOM-free core tested in Node, and holds a typical lesson page near 0.3–0.4 MB gzipped before content. Three caveats bound everything that follows: this environment's network policy refused every USU host, so USU facts come from search-result snippets graded by evidence level and the course rows of two credit blocks were never recovered; the user's original brief was cut off midway through its user-interface section, at "Interactive Lesson Player", so any later interface requirements are unknown; and nothing is built yet, so every lesson in the manifest is "planned".

## Twenty-two course maps form one prerequisite graph

Each course map was reconciled against sources that could be read in full: OpenStax's calculus, precalculus and University Physics books (read from OpenStax's own GitHub source), MIT 18.06 from Spring 2025 with Strang's sixth edition, Lebl's *Notes on Diffy Qs*, and USU's own syllabi and instructor pages, with Griffiths, Taylor, Schroeder, Hecht and Torre as the upper-division spines ([Calculus Vol. 2 collection](https://github.com/openstax/osbooks-calculus-bundle/blob/main/collections/calculus-volume-2.collection.xml); [mitmath/1806](https://github.com/mitmath/1806); [Lebl](https://github.com/jirilebl/diffyqs)). Module codes are unique within a course (M0-n; C1-n for MATH 1210, C2-n, C3-n; LA-n; DE-n; P-n for MATH 2250; course-local codes in physics), leaf lessons run 3–8 minutes, and tags mark [C] core, [O] optional at USU, [E] enrichment and [USU] where USU places content differently from the textbook. Physics misconceptions come from physics-education research and carry citations. The mathematics misconception lists were synthesized from teaching practice, because no mathematics-education research could be retrieved, and must be checked before they drive distractors. A ten-idea **GR spine** runs through the math core so that every course has an explicit bridge toward spacetime: differentials, the line element, Jacobians, the gradient as a covector, transformation laws, duals and signature, rapidity, ODE linearization, curvature of curves and surfaces, and PDEs.

The tracks open at very different times. Floating point, root-finding, quadrature, the first ODE solvers, proof, complex numbers, Fourier series and the Gamma function need only Calculus II and Python, so they belong in the current term. Tensors (A4, A6, A9) need partial derivatives and Jacobians, and should wait for a short multivariable primer. Differential geometry follows track A and MATH 2210, and the PDE capstone comes last, after constrained dynamics and convergence testing.

The F14 geodesic lab is the hinge between the mathematics and the GR pathway. Its first level integrates the reduced radial equations r̈ = −M/r² + L²/r³ − 3ML²/r⁴ and φ̇ = L/r² with three methods: Euler, to show failure; RK4, which is accurate but drifts; and velocity Verlet, which bounds the energy error but adds a spurious precession. It checks the precession per orbit against both the exact elliptic value and 6πM/p. Two pitfalls deserve their own lessons. Convergence tests must compare runs at identical final times. And every integrator must run in float64, which JavaScript numbers already are ([ECMA-262](https://tc39.es/ecma262/#sec-ecmascript-language-types-number-type)). The repository's `geodesic/` project already uses a fourth-order Yoshida integrator and records that "symplecticity beats order" for long orbits; `worldline/` integrates in units of r_s, where the ISCO sits at 3. The Atlas should reuse those lessons but not that code, because projects here share none.

## Thirty-seven steps reach the field equations by two routes and four honesty badges

The brief's 37 steps are a sound dependency order for Derivation Mode. They open physics-first, in Hartle's style; build the mathematical core in Carroll's order; then move to solutions and the frontier ([Hartle](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/gr-qc/0506075); [Carroll](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/9712019)). Two weaknesses matter for this learner. Steps 10–19 put ten abstract lessons between the metric and any black hole. And the solutions block teaches horizons (22) before the weak-field tests (23–25), and gravitational waves (27) before their derivation (28).

The fix keeps all 37 nodes and their numbers and changes only the routes. In Intuition Mode, geodesics are taught as "maximal aging" straight after proper time. That is justified because, in the Newtonian limit, everyday gravity lives in g_tt and free particles follow worldlines of maximal proper time ([Pössel 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.11589)). Steps 10, 11, 13 and 17 become short concept cards rather than omitted lessons. No published physics-education study compares physics-first and math-first GR teaching, so this ordering is reasoned, not empirically validated.

Fourteen equations get symbol-by-symbol dissections in the same convention set: the Einstein field equations with Λ; the geodesic equation; the Christoffel symbols; the Riemann tensor; the Ricci tensor and scalar; the Einstein tensor; the perfect-fluid stress-energy tensor; the Schwarzschild, Kerr (Boyer–Lindquist) and FLRW metrics; the Friedmann, acceleration and continuity equations; the linearized field equations in Lorenz gauge; the quadrupole formula; and the Schwarzschild effective potentials.

Each is a card with a symbol table (meaning, units, range), sign traps, what the equation accomplishes, and at least one check the notes ran, and every card shows a convention badge, because textbooks differ in up to three signs and those differences are the commonest source of "wrong" answers. The field-equation card shows the pattern: it presents G_μν + Λg_μν = (8πG/c⁴)T_μν with G_μν ≡ R_μν − ½Rg_μν, explains that these ten equations leave only six independent because ∇^μG_μν ≡ 0, an identity that in turn forces ∇^μT_μν = 0, and names the trap that the equation is not "curvature = mass", because pressure also gravitates through ρ + 3p/c² ([Carroll §4.4](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/9712019); [Baez & Bunn](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/0103044)). This format meets the learner's request that every new symbol and every variable be explained, and each symbol also gets a hover card giving its name, how to say it, its type, its meaning and the lesson where it first appears.

Five authoring rules make the badges enforceable: never write "possible" for an amber item without "no known physical way" beside it; give every number a source and a "checked on" date; show both camps' numbers for every open tension; place badges inline, at the sentence they govern; and quote press-release language only inside a "how it was reported vs what was shown" box.

The energy-conditions lesson is the judging tool for steps 34–36. The cosmological constant satisfies the null energy condition (ρc² + p = 0), so dark energy is not "exotic matter"; the strong condition is the one cosmology already violates; and the null condition is the one wormholes and warp drives must violate. The warp-drive literature is clear enough to teach plainly. Alcubierre's metric is correct mathematics ([Alcubierre](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/0009013)). The 2021 positive-energy superluminal claims checked only Eulerian observers and fall to the generic violation of the null energy condition. The subluminal, constant-velocity "physical warp drive" shells satisfy the energy conditions but are inertial shells of matter with no way to accelerate and no faster-than-light travel ([Fuchs et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.02709)). A February 2026 review finds viability "highly challenging" for reasons beyond the energy conditions ([Barzegar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16495)), and a 2021 Casimir-cavity paper widely reported as a "warp bubble" was nothing of the kind ([EPJC](https://epjc.epj.org/articles/epjc/abs/2021/07/10052_2021_Article_9484/10052_2021_Article_9484.html)). **There is no known physical route to faster-than-light travel**, and the Atlas presents wormholes and warp drives as amber geometries that teach energy conditions, never as technology.

Numerical relativity should be taught by replaying catalog waveforms rather than by running toy codes live, from Pretorius's 2005 breakthrough ([gr-qc/0507014](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/0507014)) and the moving-puncture method ([Campanelli et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/0511048)) to the SXS catalog. Visuals should drop the rubber sheet as an explanation, because it shows only spatial curvature while everyday gravity lives mostly in g_tt ([Pössel](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.11589); [Kersting & Steier](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11191-018-9997-4)), in favour of computed views with mandatory labels, such as a maximal-aging worldline editor captioned "clock-rate differences exaggerated ×N". The repository's `geodesic/` sandbox (95 checks), `blackhole/` ray tracer and `worldline/` film are precedents, and `blackhole/`'s Kerr–Schild integrator can render step 24's lensing. Question banks should grade only verified, stable values (the classic tests, pulsar timing, GW150914, Planck 2018, the ISCO formulas), badge dated values such as H₀ and catalog counts as OPEN, and leave out four claims the notes could not verify: a "10 km/day" GPS error figure, a claim that Cassini's error was underestimated, a 2025 Cygnus X-1 mass, and a gas-dynamics mass for M87*.

## Mastery comes from evidence while XP rewards effort

The brief's lesson shape matches the strongest learning-science findings, and four upgrades strengthen it: faded worked examples, interleaved later practice, implicitly scaffolded visuals and an optional "try first" step. Practice testing and spacing are the best-supported study techniques ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)). Repeated testing gave 61% recall after a week against 40% for restudying ([Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)). Blocked practice scored 89% during practice but 20% a week later, against 60% and 63% for interleaved practice ([Rohrer & Taylor 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8)).

An Atlas lesson therefore runs 8–15 minutes: a short intro that names new terms and symbols first and retrieves one prerequisite; one manipulable visual with a goal prompt and PhET-style implicit scaffolding ([Wieman et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1161948)); an optional, unscored "try first" challenge for conceptual lessons only; one worked example revealed step by step with subgoal labels; four to eight practice items that fade from a completion problem to full problems and end on a discrimination item; an unassisted, mixed checkpoint; and spaced review. Worked examples help novices ([Barbieri et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1)) but become redundant for experts ([Kalyuga et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4)), so a learner already Proficient on the prerequisites can skip straight to the checkpoint. The math notes' 3–8-minute leaf topics are the content units that fill this 8–15-minute cycle, not a competing lesson length.

| Component | Specification | Evidence |
|---|---|---|

Engagement design follows self-determination theory: every game element must inform rather than control ([Ryan & Deci 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68)). The evidence for caution is consistent. Gamification's average effects are small to medium (cognitive g = 0.49, motivational 0.36, behavioural 0.25) and less stable in rigorous studies ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)). Expected tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation ([Deci et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)). A semester of badges, leaderboards and competition lowered both motivation and exam scores ([Hanus & Fox 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2014.08.019)). Duolingo learners practised to keep a meter gold rather than to review what they needed ([Settles & Meeder 2016](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/P16-1174)). That is why the map, not the XP bar, is the main display.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| XP | 10 per practice item after a genuine attempt, 15 per spaced-review item, 5 per worked example, 40 per checkpoint, and 150 per boss attempt, pass or fail. No accuracy bonus. Diminishing returns after about 20 items on one skill in a day |
| Skill map | The open learner model: each skill's state shown by colour and shape, a gentle "review due" glint, the ready-to-learn frontier highlighted, and artwork revealed as regions are charted |
| Boss battles | Built by evidence-centred design: claims, then evidence, then tasks ([Mislevy et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1002/j.2333-8504.2003.tb01908.x)). 3–5 multi-part problems, including a context-rich problem with no cue to the principle, a two-unit mix, a two-tier explanation, a find-the-error item and a categorisation item. A claim passes at about 70% of its evidence, which is a convention, not an evidence-based value. No lives or timers; "I'm stuck, reveal" turns a part into practice |

| Never | Leaderboards, hearts or lives, loot boxes, timers on learning, loss-framed warnings, confirm-shaming, paid streak repair, and any AI helper that hands out answers: unrestricted GPT-4 help raised practice scores but lowered later exam scores ([Bastani et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)) |

None of this should claim more precision than it has. No published study tests this exact combination of Elo, evidence gates and FSRS; FSRS's defaults come from flashcard data, so its computed intervals (2.3, 11.9, 49.7, 173.8 and 527 days when every review is graded Good) may be wrong for procedural skills; and the thresholds are design choices checked only by simulation ([learning_model scripts](</home/user/Projects/research_notes/Physics Atlas curriculum and design/verification/learning_model/>)), although that simulation does show the useful property that hinted solves alone plateau near p̂ ≈ 0.68 and never reach Proficient. The Atlas should therefore run a local calibration audit, raising the threshold or lowering K if Mastered skills succeed less than 85% of the time on their first spaced review over 20 or more cases, and should phrase every state as an estimate ("The Atlas estimates…") with a "Why this state?" panel. One interface caveat applies throughout: the brief stopped at "Interactive Lesson Player", so the player described here is inferred from the lesson template and should be confirmed with the user.

The answer checker is the heart of the build. It should be written in-house on math.js, following WeBWorK's defaults: five random points in [−2, 2], a relative tolerance of 10⁻⁴, and absolute comparison near zero ([PG Formula.pm](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openwebwork/pg/main/lib/Value/Formula.pm)). A prototype ran 1,000 checks in 69 ms in Node and exposed the two traps the design must close. First, log(x²) and 2 log x "matched" only because negative samples went complex and were skipped. Second, an antiderivative that differed by a constant failed. The design closes both: authors declare a domain for each variable and an answer mode (up to an additive constant, modulo 2π, equation equivalence, or a required form checked on the parse tree); units are checked with `equalBase` before values are compared in SI; significant figures are counted from the learner's string; and seeds make every variant reproducible. The same checker validates content in Node, where every key must pass and every distractor must fail and differ from every other.

Python runs in Pyodide inside a module worker, because version 314.0.0 dropped classic workers ([changelog](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pyodide/pyodide/main/docs/project/changelog.md)). A cold start took 3.7 s on localhost. The Stop button terminates and respawns the worker. True interrupts need a SharedArrayBuffer and cross-origin isolation. GitHub Pages can provide isolation only if the Atlas's own service worker injects COOP and COEP headers, at the cost of a reload on the first visit ([coi-serviceworker](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gzuidhof/coi-serviceworker/master/README.md)).

Progress lives in IndexedDB under a `physics-atlas` namespace, as an append-only attempt log. Each entry records the item, variant seed, answer, correctness, latency, hints and time. Mastery, schedules and achievements are recomputed from the log. The store has a schema version, its migrations are pure functions tested in Node, and JSON export and import merge by event id.

That matters because every project in this repository shares the origin daxjdavis526.github.io. Quotas and eviction apply to the whole origin, and Safari deletes script-written data after seven days without interaction ([MDN](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdn/content/main/files/en-us/web/api/storage_api/storage_quotas_and_eviction_criteria/index.md)). The Atlas should call `navigator.storage.persist()` after the diagnostic and remind the learner to back up.

One hand-written service worker lives in the Atlas folder, and its default scope is that folder. It precaches a versioned shell, listed in a module that a Node test checks. It serves versioned vendor paths cache-first and content stale-while-revalidate, and it offers "Make this course available offline". It updates only through a "new version ready" prompt, never in the middle of a lesson ([MDN](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdn/content/main/files/en-us/web/api/service_worker_api/using_service_workers/index.md)).

Routing uses hash URLs, because Pages returns 404 for client-side routes on a fresh load ([spa-github-pages](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rafgraph/spa-github-pages/gh-pages/README.md)). The app keeps a single import map, because Firefox supports more than one only behind a flag ([browser-compat-data](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/main/html/elements/script.json)).

Accessibility targets WCAG 2.2 AA ([dragging movements](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/w3c/wcag/main/guidelines/sc/22/dragging-movements.html)): a non-drag alternative for every drag (2.5.7), targets of at least 24 × 24 CSS pixels (2.5.8), focus never hidden by sticky UI (2.4.11), contrast of 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for graphics checked by a Node test over the colour tokens, reduced-motion support, and every canvas paired with a caption, a live readout and a data table generated from the same model. MathJax 4 stays an optional enhanced-accessibility mode only, because its default configuration fetches fonts from jsDelivr, which breaks offline use and the no-CDN rule.

The ranked technical risks are, in order: turning MathLive's LaTeX or ASCIIMath output into checker input; Pyodide's compiled wheel sizes and first-run latency on real networks, neither yet measured; cross-origin isolation on Pages, which is optional because terminate-and-respawn always works; Safari's seven-day eviction, mitigated by export and `persist()`; and three.js after r185, since r186 ships no minified files ([npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/three)).

## Conclusion

The cheapest next step is neither more research nor more code. It is two pasted catalog tabs. The requirements and degree-map pages would turn every "reconstructed fill" row into an A-level record, settle whether PHYS 3710 and MATH 2250 count toward this emphasis, and let the manifest's labels be finalized in one pass. Until then, the honest presentation is the one recommended here: credit blocks shown as verified, course rows as reconstructed.

The deeper finding is that the Atlas needs one "honesty grammar" across three layers that are usually designed separately: evidence letters for what USU requires, status badges for what physics has established, and evidence-gated states for what the learner has mastered. Each answers the same question, namely what was actually shown and by what evidence, and each should open into a "why this label" panel with sources and dates. The DOM-free checker and validators should be built first, because the top technical risk and every honesty rule are enforced there. Built that way, the Atlas can let this learner reach black holes this semester through shared Intuition-Mode lessons, without ever overstating what the learner, the catalog or the physics has proved.
