## K1. Problem solutions

### Conceptual

**C1 — widening the envelope from (−40, +60) to (−54, +71) °C.**
In order of severity:

1. **Case mass, via MEOP.** The hot limit moves from ΔT = +39 K to +50 K above
   the +21 °C reference. With πK = 3.08×10⁻³ K⁻¹ the hot-limit pressure factor
   rises from 1.128 to 1.166 — about 3.4 % more pressure, and the case, joints,
   igniter closure and nozzle attachment all scale with it. This is the term
   that consumes the most mass, because it multiplies the single heaviest inert
   item.
2. **Grain structural margin at cold.** The cold case moves from ΔT = −61 K to
   −75 K below the reference, and it moves *toward* Tg, where strain capability
   collapses. This is the requirement most likely to fail outright rather than
   merely cost mass, and the fix (a softer, higher-elongation binder) usually
   costs performance.
3. **Ignition system.** Igniter output must now light a colder grain, which
   pushes igniter mass up, which worsens the hot-limit ignition overpressure —
   a two-sided squeeze.
4. **Sustain-phase / low-pressure margin** (if the motor has one) and the
   dispersion budget: burn time now varies over a factor of 1.47 across the
   envelope instead of 1.36.

The mass answer is the case. A student who says "the dispersion budget" has
identified a real consequence but not the expensive one.

**C2 — why low n is good for ballistics and bad for throttling.**
Two sensitivities, both governed by n. From Eq. 3.1, any perturbation in
burning area, burn-rate coefficient, throat area or temperature is amplified in
pressure by 1/(1−n): low n means a stiff, well-behaved motor whose pressure
does not chase manufacturing tolerance, nozzle erosion or soak temperature.
From Eq. 3.9, thrust responds to throat area as A_t^{−n/(1−n)}: low n means
almost no thrust authority from throat motion. With n = 0.35, halving the
throat area changes p_c by 2.9× but thrust by only 1.45×; with n = 0.7 the same
motion gives 10× pressure and 5× thrust — controllable, and ballistically
fragile everywhere else. You cannot have both from one propellant, which is why
throttleable-solid work either accepts a high-n propellant with its stability
problems or accepts a small thrust range.

**C3 — the dual-pulse barrier.**
Functions: (i) carry pulse-1 pressure and the pulse-1 thermal environment
without deflecting into or damaging the pulse-2 grain; (ii) thermally insulate
the pulse-2 grain during pulse 1 and the coast; (iii) open on command,
promptly and repeatably, without producing debris the nozzle cannot tolerate.
The hardest to demonstrate over decades is **(iii)**: opening is a
fracture/rupture event whose threshold depends on material properties that age,
and it must be repeatable at both temperature extremes after twenty years of
thermal cycling — and it can only be verified destructively, one article at a
time, on a surveillance sample. Pressure capability, by contrast, is a
straightforward structural analysis with a proof test behind it. A student who
answers "(ii)" has a defensible second place, since the heat-soak effect on the
pulse-2 grain is also hard to bound; the winning argument for (iii) is that
verification is destructive and statistical rather than analytical.

**C4 — smoke.**
Primary smoke is condensed material generated by the motor itself: metal oxide
particulate. Its quantity is set by the formulation, so it is deterministic and
verifiable in a single test. Secondary smoke is atmospheric water condensing on
hygroscopic exhaust species; whether it forms at all depends on ambient
temperature and relative humidity. The requirement must therefore be written
against a stated atmospheric condition (or a percentile of conditions), and
verification requires either testing at that condition or a validated model
plus a measurement of the hygroscopic species. Writing "no visible trail"
without an atmospheric qualifier is an unverifiable requirement.

**C5 — slow cook-off can be worse than fast cook-off.**
In fast cook-off the article is engulfed and the case fails early and locally
from external heating, venting the propellant before the bulk reaches a
reactive state. In slow cook-off the *whole* charge is brought slowly and
nearly uniformly to a temperature at which self-heating chemistry begins, with
the case still intact and strong; the reaction then starts in a confined,
pre-heated, large mass, and confinement is what turns burning into
deflagration or explosion. Design features: (a) thermally activated venting —
melting plugs, pressure-relief features, joints or case-wall features designed
to open at a temperature below the propellant's reaction onset; (b) propellant
and binder selection for a benign self-heating and decomposition behaviour;
(c) insulation and case-material choice that avoids holding pressure at
temperature (a composite case degrades progressively and vents; a
high-strength steel case does not).

**C6 — mass fraction vs volumetric loading.**
ζ = m_p/m_0 is a *mass* ratio and is what the rocket equation uses; it is
driven by the Δv or range requirement. η_V = V_p/V_c is a *volume* ratio and is
driven by the external envelope — launcher tube, silo length, airframe
diameter. Conflict: a grain geometry that raises η_V (end-burner, thick-web
high-loading bore) generally has a poorer thrust-time shape and often needs
more insulation, because more of the case is exposed to hot gas for longer,
which costs inert mass and therefore ζ. Choosing a star or finocyl for trace
neutrality costs η_V through the unburned slivers and the larger initial port.

**C7 — the 18-year fleet leader.**
Required: (i) the current cold-day grain structural analysis re-run with the
*aged* mechanical properties, not the delivery ones, to see whether the margin
still closes at −54 °C; (ii) a cumulative-damage assessment over the article's
actual thermal history, not a single-point check; (iii) a trend fit of measured
strain capability against age with its uncertainty, extrapolated to 25 years
with a stated confidence bound. Missing data: how the property is trending
between measurements (a two-point fit cannot distinguish a linear from an
accelerating loss); the *distribution* of the property across the fleet, not
just the leader's value; the real thermal exposure of the fleet versus the
leader; and bond-line data, since the leader's grain properties say nothing
about the propellant–liner interface, which is the other failure path.

**C8 — hybrid ground safety and what it costs.**
The fuel grain alone contains no oxidiser: it will not sustain combustion, it
is not an energetic material for classification purposes, it can be
manufactured without an energetic-materials facility, and shock or fragment
impact cannot propagate a reaction through it. That removes most of §3.8's
problem at a stroke. The cost is architectural: the oxidiser needs a tank, a
feed system, a valve and a pressurisation system, all of which are inert mass
that a solid does not carry; and the low regression rate (WE3: ~1–2 mm/s
against 8–10 mm/s) forces long grains or multi-port geometries, which drop
volumetric loading to roughly 0.5–0.7 and leave unburned slivers. A hybrid of
equal impulse is bigger and heavier, and it now has an oxidiser with its own
storage, compatibility and leak requirements.

### Calculation

**N1.** πK = σ_p/(1−n) = 0.0025/0.55 = **4.55×10⁻³ K⁻¹**.
Over ΔT = 125 K: p ratio = exp(4.545×10⁻³ × 125) = exp(0.568) = **1.77**.
Baseline (0.002, 0.35): πK = 3.08×10⁻³, ratio = exp(0.385) = **1.47**.
Attribution: raising σ_p from 0.0020 to 0.0025 alone (n = 0.35) gives πK =
3.85×10⁻³ and a ratio of 1.62; raising n from 0.35 to 0.45 alone (σ_p = 0.002)
gives πK = 3.64×10⁻³ and a ratio of 1.58. The two changes do comparable damage
here, with σ_p slightly worse — but note that the n effect accelerates: another
0.1 on n costs far more than the first 0.1 did, because 1/(1−n) is convex. The
expected wrong answer is "n matters more because it is an exponent"; it is an
exponent on pressure, not on temperature, and it enters temperature sensitivity
only through the 1/(1−n) amplification.

**N2.** ΔT = −75 K: factor exp(−0.2325) = 0.7925 →
p_c = **7.13 MPa**, action time = 9.0/0.7925 = **11.4 s**.
ΔT = +50 K: factor exp(0.155) = 1.1677 →
p_c = **10.5 MPa**, action time = 9.0/1.1677 = **7.71 s**.
MEOP = 10.51 × 1.10 = 11.56 MPa; required burst = 11.56 × 1.25 =
**14.4 MPa**, i.e. 1.61 × the nominal +21 °C operating pressure. That factor —
"the case is designed to about 1.6 times the pressure you actually operate at"
— is the number to remember.

**N3.** Area ratio: from Eq. 3.8 with equal a, A_{b,b}/A_{b,s} = (F_b/F_s)^{1−n}
= 6^{0.60} = **2.93**.
Sustain pressure = 11.0/6 = **1.83 MPa**.
Cold day: 1.833 × exp(−3.1×10⁻³ × 75) = 1.833 × 0.7925 = **1.45 MPa**, which is
**below the 1.8 MPa low-pressure limit**. The design does not close. Options:
reduce the thrust ratio; raise the boost pressure (heavier case); use a
propellant with a lower stable-combustion limit; or accept a reduced cold
operating limit. This is WE2's lesson repeated with different numbers, and it
is the intended answer — the arithmetic is easy and the conclusion is a
failure.

**N4.** K_n = p_c^{1−n}/(a ρ_p c*) = (9.0×10⁶)^{0.65}/(3.216×10⁻⁵ × 1770 ×
1520) = 3.313×10⁴/86.5 = **383**.
A_b = 383 × 1.20×10⁻³ = **0.460 m²**.
r = a p_c^n = 3.216×10⁻⁵ × (9×10⁶)^{0.35} = **8.74 mm/s**.
ṁ = ρ_p A_b r = 1770 × 0.4595 × 8.736×10⁻³ = **7.11 kg/s**.
F = C_F p_c A_t = 1.55 × 9.0×10⁶ × 1.20×10⁻³ = **16.7 kN**.
Cross-check: F = ṁ C_F c* = 7.11 × 1.55 × 1520 = 16.8 kN ✓.

**N5.** D = 0.12 m: A = 1.131×10⁻² m², G_ox = 176.8 kg m⁻² s⁻¹,
ṙ = 1.0×10⁻⁴ × 176.8^{0.55} = **1.72 mm/s**,
ṁ_f = 930 × π × 0.12 × 1.2 × 1.7225×10⁻³ = **0.725 kg/s**, O/F = **2.76**.
D = 0.20 m: G_ox = 63.7, ṙ = **0.982 mm/s**, ṁ_f = **0.689 kg/s**,
O/F = **2.90**.
Shift: O/F **rises** (fuel flow falls) because n = 0.55 > 0.5, so
ṁ_f ∝ D^{1−2n} = D^{−0.10}. Check: (0.20/0.12)^{−0.10} = 0.950, and
0.7247 × 0.950 = 0.689 ✓. Note how small the shift is — 5 % in fuel flow for a
1.67× diameter change — precisely because n is close to 0.5.

**N6.** N = ln(1−C)/ln(R) = ln(0.05)/ln(0.995) = (−2.996)/(−0.005013) = **598**
firings at 95 % confidence; ln(0.20)/ln(0.995) = **322** at 80 %.
Comment: the confidence level is worth a factor of 1.86 in cost here, and it is
a *negotiated* parameter, not a physical one. That is why reliability claims
must always be quoted with their confidence, and why programmes under cost
pressure argue about confidence levels rather than about reliability. The
deeper point is that neither number is affordable, which forces the
variables-data and physics-of-failure approach of §3.7.

**N7.** m_0 = 76.1/0.72 = **106 kg**; inert = 106 − 76.1 = **29.6 kg**.
At ζ = 0.94: m_0 = 76.1/0.94 = **81.0 kg**, inert = **4.86 kg**.
The difference — 29.6 kg against 4.9 kg of inert mass for the same propellant —
is the small-motor scaling penalty of §3.6: case wall at a handling-driven
minimum thickness, insulation at a practical minimum, and nearly fixed masses
for the nozzle, igniter, S&A and closures. Inert mass scales closer to surface
area than to volume, so ζ falls as the motor shrinks. The Star 48B's 0.94 is
not available at tactical scale at any price, and a proposal that assumes it is
should be rejected on the first page.

**N8.** Single pulse: m_p = 0.80 × 100 = 80.0 kg. Dual pulse at ζ = 0.76:
m_p = 76.0 kg. Propellant lost = **4.0 kg**.
ΔI_t = m_p g_0 I_sp = 4.0 × 9.80665 × 250 = **9.81 kN·s** given up (out of
80 × 9.80665 × 250 = 196 kN·s, i.e. 5 %).
What it buys: a commanded second ignition after an arbitrary coast — energy
management. Whether 5 % of total impulse is worth it is a mission-level
question: it is worth it when the vehicle's effectiveness depends on *when* the
second impulse is delivered rather than on how much of it there is.

### Engineering reasoning

**R1.** Pressure ratio = 11.4/7.9 = 1.443 over ΔT = 95 K, so
πK = ln(1.443)/95 = **3.86×10⁻³ K⁻¹**, and σ_p = πK(1−n) = 3.86×10⁻³ × 0.65 =
**2.51×10⁻³ K⁻¹**.
Consistency check: the action-time ratio should be the inverse pressure ratio,
1.443; measured is 11.2/8.0 = 1.400, a 3 % discrepancy, easily accounted for by
throat erosion (which reduces the effective pressure ratio late in the burn),
by the difference between peak and mean pressure, and by measurement
uncertainty. Total impulse agreeing within 1 % confirms that the same
propellant mass burned in both — i.e. this is a *rate* effect, not a
burning-area effect. The motor is behaving as designed for a propellant with
σ_p ≈ 0.0025, which is at the upper end of the normal AP/HTPB band.
Most valuable further measurement: **the actual soak temperature history of
each article** (were they fully thermally equilibrated? a partially soaked
large grain has a radially varying burn rate and biases σ_p), followed by
throat-area measurement before and after each firing to separate erosion from
ballistics.

**R2.** Pressure high, action time short, **and impulse high** — the third
number is the diagnostic. Temperature alone cannot raise total impulse; it
redistributes the same propellant mass in time. Impulse up by 4 % with pressure
up 9 % means *more propellant burned, faster*: additional burning area. The
hypothesis is a **grain defect that exposed extra surface** — a bore crack, a
debond along the liner exposing the outer grain surface, or a mis-cast/void
condition. (A secondary hypothesis is a mis-machined throat: a small A_t raises
pressure and shortens action time, but it does not raise total impulse, so it
is refuted by the same number.)
NDE requested on the remaining lot: computed tomography or radiography for
cracks and voids through the web, and ultrasonic bond-line inspection at the
case–liner–propellant interfaces, with particular attention to the ends and any
geometric discontinuity. Confirmation: cracks or unbonds found in siblings, or
a recovered post-fire article showing an anomalous burn pattern. Refutation:
clean NDE across the lot plus a verified throat dimension, which would push the
investigation toward the propellant mix itself — a high-a batch would raise
pressure and shorten time, but again would not raise impulse, so a genuinely
high impulse with clean NDE means the *impulse measurement* or the propellant
mass record should be audited.

**R3.** Dimension by dimension: B wins on delivered performance (+13 s, ~5 %)
and on signature (minimum smoke against a metallised, chloride-producing
propellant). A wins on temperature sensitivity (πK: A = 0.0018/0.70 =
2.57×10⁻³ K⁻¹; B = 0.0035/0.45 = **7.78×10⁻³ K⁻¹**, a factor of three worse —
across a 125 K envelope that is a pressure ratio of 2.65 against 1.38, which
is an enormous case-mass penalty and an enormous dispersion), on ballistic
stability (n = 0.30 vs 0.55), on insensitivity (nitramine systems are harder to
make IM-compliant), on combustion stability margin (metal damping), and on
cost and supply chain.
(a) **Launcher booster: B.** The envelope is conditioned, so πK barely matters;
signature and IM are not requirements; the 5 % Isp is real money. The one
reservation is stability and cost.
(b) **Stored field motor with a signature requirement: A, or a third
candidate.** B's πK of 7.8×10⁻³ K⁻¹ is disqualifying for a −54…+71 °C
requirement on case mass alone, before the IM assessment. The honest answer is
that A fails the signature requirement and B fails the envelope requirement, so
the correct engineering move is to reject the binary choice and go back with a
reduced-smoke, low-n candidate — and, if none exists, to negotiate either the
signature requirement or the temperature envelope. Recognising that the
question is a false dichotomy is worth more marks than picking either option.

**R4.** Questions to ask: What is the required coast duration, and what is the
pulse-2 grain's predicted bulk temperature at the end of it (and hence the
pulse-2 ballistic shift by πK)? What is the barrier concept, and what is its
demonstrated opening-pressure repeatability at both temperature extremes? What
is the barrier's mass and volume, and what is the resulting ζ and η_V against
the boost–sustain baseline? What is the pulse-2 ignition system's reliability,
and how does the *product* of two ignition reliabilities compare with the
single-pulse baseline? What new items enter the surveillance programme, and how
will barrier function be verified on aged articles? What does the nozzle see if
the barrier is frangible?
Evidence needed on the barrier: opening-pressure test data across the
temperature envelope with a real distribution, not a mean; aged-article opening
tests, or at minimum accelerated-aged specimens with a stated shift factor and
its justification; thermal analysis and instrumented firings showing the
pulse-2 grain's temperature history through pulse 1 and the maximum coast; and
nozzle-tolerance evidence for any debris.

**R5.** The counter-argument, requirement by requirement: **response time** —
a hybrid must open a valve, establish oxidiser flow and then ignite, so its
ignition-to-thrust time is longer and its dispersion worse than a solid's
single-digit milliseconds; **volumetric loading** — multi-port grains plus an
oxidiser tank and feed system put a hybrid at roughly 0.5–0.7 against a solid's
0.85–0.95, so it does not fit the envelope; **mass fraction** — tank, valve,
pressurisation and feed hardware are inert mass a solid does not carry;
**environmental survivability and storage** — a stored liquid oxidiser brings
leakage, seal aging, material compatibility, freezing and vapour-pressure
limits across a −54…+71 °C envelope, which is a harder storage problem than a
cured grain, not an easier one; **production rate** — hybrids have no
rate-production base comparable to solid motor plants.
Where the hybrid nevertheless wins: when the mission needs genuine in-flight
throttling or shutdown; when ground and platform safety dominates the
requirement set (crewed vehicles, shared magazines); when the article is
produced in small numbers so rate production is irrelevant; and when the
envelope is generous enough that the volumetric penalty does not bind. Those
are exactly the conditions of the flying suborbital example in §6.6.

## K2. Quiz answers

**Q1 (8) — (c) 0.006 K⁻¹.** πK = σ_p/(1−n) = 0.003/0.5. (a) is σ_p/2 — the
student has multiplied by (1−n) instead of dividing. (b) forgets the
amplification entirely. (d) uses (1−n) = 0.667.

**Q2 (8) — (b) MEOP relative to nominal pressure.** A conditioned booster's
propellant sits in a narrow temperature band, so the hot-day pressure excursion
it must design for is small; the field motor must add a full πK×ΔT excursion.
(a) is a combustion and injector-free property, essentially unaffected by soak
temperature; (c) is an ambient-pressure and envelope question, not a
temperature one; (d) actually favours neither strongly, and if anything a large
booster needs more ignition energy, not less.

**Q3 (12).** ΔT = +50 K. Factor = exp(2.8×10⁻³ × 50) = exp(0.140) = 1.150.
p_c = 9.0 × 1.150 = **10.4 MPa**. Action time = 6.0/1.150 = **5.22 s**.
Marks: 4 for the exponential, 4 for the pressure, 4 for recognising that the
time scales inversely (a student who multiplies the time by 1.150 has the
physics backwards and loses all four).

**Q4 (8) — (c) 8.4.** Thrust ratio = (area ratio)^{1/(1−n)} = 4^{1/0.65} =
4^{1.538} = 8.44. (a) is the area ratio itself — the student has forgotten the
amplification. (b) is 4^{1/(1+n)}. (d) is 4^2.

**Q5 (10).** Stimuli: **fast cook-off, slow cook-off, bullet impact, fragment
impact, sympathetic reaction, shaped-charge jet impact** (2 points each up to
10, capped). Unacceptable response levels under every stimulus: **detonation
(Type I) and partial detonation (Type II)** — and explosion (Type III) is
unacceptable under most. Full marks for naming detonation and partial
detonation; a student who names only "detonation" gets partial credit.

**Q6 (12).** n = 0.7 > 0.5, so ṁ_f ∝ D^{1−2n} = D^{−0.4}: fuel flow **falls**
and with constant oxidiser flow **O/F rises** through the burn.
Ratio = (0.20/0.10)^{−0.4} = 2^{−0.4} = **0.758**, i.e. fuel flow drops 24 %
and O/F rises by 1/0.758 = 32 %. Marks: 4 for the direction, 4 for the exponent
rule, 4 for the number.

**Q7 (8) — (c).** Secondary smoke is atmospheric condensation on exhaust
nuclei. (a) describes primary smoke only. (b) inverts the two — it is
*secondary* smoke that is humidity-dependent. (d) is the common error: removing
aluminium removes primary smoke but leaves chloride-driven secondary smoke.

**Q8 (12).** R_LB = (1−C)^{1/N} = 0.10^{1/60} = exp(−2.3026/60) =
**0.962**.
Higher claimed reliability is legitimate because (i) the claim uses *variables*
data — measured margins such as burst pressure minus MEOP — which extract far
more information per article than pass/fail, and (ii) it incorporates
physics-of-failure analysis, lot-acceptance process control and surveillance
trends, so it is a claim about analysed mechanisms rather than a black-box
frequency. It might legitimately be **lower** because the 60 articles are not
independent: they share lots, mixes, liner batches and operators, so a
correlated failure mechanism inside a lot means N overstates the information
content — and because the fielded article, after storage and transport, is not
the article that was fired.

**Q9 (12).** Factor = exp(3.0×10⁻³ × 25) = exp(0.075) = 1.078, so pulse-2
chamber pressure is about **7.8 % high** (and pulse-2 burn time about 7 %
short). Consequences not captured by πK: the heat soak is **non-uniform**, so
the grain has a temperature gradient and therefore a spatially varying burn
rate rather than a single shifted value; thermal stress and possible mechanical
damage or debonding at the heated face; degradation of the pulse-2 propellant's
mechanical properties or, in the limit, autoignition; and softening or creep of
the barrier material itself before it is asked to open. Any one of these earns
the final marks.

**Q10 (12).** **Trace A**: pressure up, time down, impulse unchanged — the same
propellant mass burned faster. That is a **rate** effect, overwhelmingly likely
a higher-than-recorded soak temperature (or a high-a propellant batch).
**Trace B**: pressure up, time down, *and impulse up* — **more propellant
burned**, i.e. extra burning area: a grain crack, a liner debond exposing
additional surface, or a casting defect.
The distinguishing measurement is **total impulse itself** (equivalently, the
integral of pressure over time together with the throat area) — the first two
numbers are identical between the traces and cannot separate them. Credit is
also given for "the recorded soak temperature", which tests the A hypothesis
directly, but the single measurement that separates the two is the impulse.

## K3. Trade-study reference solution (T1)

**Requirement recap.** 200 kN·s from a fixed envelope; −54 °C to +71 °C;
25-year storage; IM requirement; reduced-smoke signature requirement.

**Recommendation: (b), the single-pulse fixed-throat motor with reduced-smoke
AP/HTPB in a carbon/epoxy case** — with one caveat stated below.

**The reasoning.**

*Signature screens out (a) immediately.* The requirement is reduced smoke;
a metallised propellant produces primary smoke by construction. No amount of
other goodness recovers that. Option (a) is therefore non-compliant, not merely
inferior.

*Temperature envelope and πK.* Take a representative reduced-smoke AP/HTPB
with σ_p = 0.0022 K⁻¹ and n = 0.35: πK = 3.38×10⁻³ K⁻¹, and across
ΔT = 125 K the pressure ratio is exp(0.423) = 1.53. Referred to a +21 °C
nominal of, say, 9 MPa, the hot limit is 9 × exp(3.38×10⁻³ × 50) = 10.7 MPa;
with a 1.10 dispersion factor and a 1.25 design factor the case must burst at
14.6 MPa — 1.63 × nominal. That is the number that sizes the case and it is
identical in kind for options (b) and (c). A candidate propellant with a
materially higher σ_p or n should be rejected on this calculation alone
(compare problem R3).

*Mass fraction estimate.* For a 200 kN·s requirement at I_sp ≈ 245 s
(reduced-smoke, no metal), propellant mass = 200 000/(9.80665 × 245) = 83.2 kg.
At a defensible tactical-scale ζ of 0.78 with a composite case, loaded mass is
83.2/0.78 = 107 kg, inert 24 kg. Option (c) at ζ ≈ 0.74 (barrier, second
igniter, extra insulation, lost η_V) gives 83.2/0.74 = 112 kg for the same
impulse, or 5 kN·s less impulse in the same 107 kg — the calculation of N8 in
different clothes. Option (d)'s hybrid, with tank, feed system and η_V ≈ 0.6,
does not fit the fixed envelope at all without a substantial diameter or length
increase; that is the disqualifying argument against it, not its Isp.

*IM.* All four options must pass; (b) and (c) share the most favourable
propellant class available under the signature constraint, and the carbon/epoxy
case is an IM *asset* (progressive strength loss in a fire promotes venting
rather than sudden burst). Option (d) is intrinsically the most benign in the
grain but introduces a stored oxidiser, which is a new hazard and a new
compatibility and leakage problem over 25 years.

*Storage, surveillance and reliability.* (b) has the simplest surveillance
argument: one grain, one bond system, one ignition system. (c) adds the barrier
— a component whose function can only be verified destructively and whose
opening threshold ages — and a second ignition system, so system reliability
becomes a product. (d) adds an entire fluid system to the surveillance
programme. Under a 25-year requirement this ordering is decisive.

*Production and cost.* (b) is a conventional rate-produced article. (c) costs
barrier qualification plus a second igniter per unit. (d) has no comparable
rate-production base.

**The caveat.** If — and only if — the mission genuinely requires commanded
energy management (a second impulse delivered at a time chosen in flight), then
(c) becomes the answer and the 5 % impulse penalty is simply the price. The
trade study must therefore first establish whether that requirement is real. If
it is not in the requirement set, (c) is buying a capability nobody asked for
with mass fraction everybody wants.

**Rubric.**

| element | marks |
|---|---|
| Screens (a) out on the signature requirement as non-compliance, not preference | 10 |
| Computes πK for a stated propellant and turns it into a MEOP/burst pressure | 15 |
| Computes propellant mass from the impulse requirement and a stated Isp | 10 |
| Estimates ζ for at least two options and compares like-for-like in the same envelope | 15 |
| Rejects (d) on volumetric loading and envelope, not on Isp | 10 |
| Treats the dual-pulse barrier as a *surveillance and reliability* item, not only a mass item | 10 |
| States the ten requirement dimensions and addresses each at least briefly | 10 |
| Identifies the largest remaining uncertainty and the measurement that reduces it (e.g. aged strain capability at −54 °C, or barrier opening repeatability) | 10 |
| Names the requirement to push back on, with a reason | 10 |

**What loses marks:** choosing (a) because it has the highest Isp (the
signature requirement is not negotiable in the problem statement); choosing (d)
because "hybrids are IM-friendly" without addressing envelope, response time
and oxidiser storage; quoting a large-motor mass fraction (0.90+) for a
tactical-scale motor; computing MEOP without the temperature excursion;
recommending (c) without first asking whether commanded second ignition is a
requirement; and any answer that treats the ten dimensions as independent
rather than trading against each other.

## K4. Common wrong answers and what they reveal

**"Total impulse rises with soak temperature."** It does not, to first order:
the same propellant mass burns, faster. Students who say this have not
internalised that πK acts on *rate*, and they will misdiagnose every static
firing trace they ever see. The corollary they also miss: an impulse anomaly is
always a mass or burning-area anomaly, never a temperature one.

**Multiplying burn time by the pressure factor instead of dividing.** A sign
error with a physical meaning: it implies a hotter motor burns longer. Always
sanity-check against mass conservation, ρ_p A_b r = p_c A_t/c*.

**Confusing σ_p with πK.** They differ by 1/(1−n), which is 1.5–2 for real
propellants. Quoting a "temperature sensitivity" without saying whether it is
at constant pressure or constant K_n is the single most common unit-level error
in this subject, and it produces answers wrong by 50 %.

**Treating the IM requirement as "will not react".** It reveals that the
student has not read what the standards actually require: a bounded *response
level*, not the absence of reaction. It leads to proposals that are physically
impossible for an article containing its own oxidiser.

**Believing that firing more motors is how reliability is bought.** Eq. 3.7's
arithmetic (229 firings for 0.99 at 90 % confidence) is a shock the first time.
Students who have not done it propose qualification programmes that are off by
two orders of magnitude in cost.

**Assuming large-motor mass fractions at small scale.** Quoting 0.92 for a
tactical motor. It reveals that ζ has been memorised as a constant rather than
understood as an area-to-volume scaling result.

**"Removing aluminium gives minimum smoke."** It gives *reduced* smoke.
Chloride-producing oxidiser still nucleates secondary smoke in humid air. The
error reveals that the two smoke mechanisms have been merged into one.

**Assuming a high-n propellant is what you want for a throttleable motor,
without noticing what n does to everything else.** Half right, and the half
that is missing — ballistic stiffness, temperature sensitivity, tolerance
amplification, stability — is why the concept remains research.

**Sizing a dual-pulse barrier as a pressure vessel only.** It reveals that the
thermal isolation and commanded-opening functions were not read as
requirements. The barrier's hardest requirement is repeatable opening after
decades, and it can only be verified destructively.

**Quoting "the Star 48B Isp" or "the Star 48B inert mass" as a single
number.** Both are contested in the open sources for reasons the course's
verification file documents (two nozzle variants; a probable transcription
error in one inert-mass figure). It reveals that the student has taken a
secondary source at face value, which is the habit this course exists to break.
